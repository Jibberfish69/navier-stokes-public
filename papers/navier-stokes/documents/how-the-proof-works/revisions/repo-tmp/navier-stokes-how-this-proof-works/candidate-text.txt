The dangerous case

Take a small tube of rotating fluid and pull it along its axis. If its length is L(t), its effective radius
is r(t), and the axial strain rate is σ(t) > 0, incompressibility gives

                                    L̇(t)                  ṙ(t)    σ(t)
                                          = σ(t),                =−      .
                                    L(t)                   r(t)      2

The tube gets longer and narrower. Rotation strengthens around a shrinking core, and the velocity
changes across a smaller distance. The kinetic energy can stay finite while the gradients rise. This
is the regularity contest in physical form: nonlinear transport can keep sharpening the fluid, while
viscosity has to smooth that concentration quickly enough to stop it.

That shrinking core is what makes scale relevant. If its width changes from ℓ to λ−1 ℓ, the Navier-
Stokes comparison is

                          uλ (x, t) = λu(λx, λ2 t),        pλ (x, t) = λ2 p(λx, λ2 t).

Local acceleration, nonlinear transport, pressure, and viscosity all gain a factor of λ3 . Going to a
finer scale strengthens nonlinear sharpening and viscous smoothing together. Scale alone does not
decide which one wins.

The Sobolev norms separate the scales more clearly:
                                                           1
                                    uλ (·, t) Ḣ s = λ,s− 2 u(·, λ2 t) Ḣ s .

Below s = 12 , concentration makes the norm shrink. Above s = 12 , it makes the norm grow. At
s = 21 , it stays level. Energy can remain finite below that height while derivatives grow above it.

A cascade through smaller cores can now sharpen the fluid without spending infinite kinetic energy.
But in order for a singularity to form, velocity has to blow up. In order for that to happen, its
acceleration would also have to blow up, which means its jerk has to blow up, and so on, and so on:

                                      u,      ∂t u,        ∂t2 u,    ...

As we climb those time derivatives toward a singularity, we should expect to see less spatial regularity,
not more.

More spatial regularity, not less

The surprise appears when we ask what each time derivative contains spatially. Every row still
carries the viscous term ν∆Vr , and

                                      ∆Vr = ∂x21 Vr + ∂x22 Vr + ∂x23 Vr .

Solving the row for its Laplacian makes the connection explicit:

One step upward in time is tied to two spatial derivatives of the response beneath it. Nonlinear
transport and pressure remain part of the row; viscosity is the term that links the temporal climb
to the spatial Sobolev scale.

This is the central reversal. A forming singularity should reveal less spatial regularity as its velocity,
acceleration, jerk, and later responses grow. The equation points toward higher Sobolev heights
instead.

We began by expecting a singularity to strip away spatial regularity as the temporal responses rose.
The construction reaches every finite spatial height and arrives at T∗ with smooth data.

The local smooth theory starts from u∗ on [T∗ , T∗ + δ]. Uniqueness joins that local solution to the
solution on [0, T∗ ], producing an extension beyond the proposed terminal time. A finite T∗ cannot
be maximal:
                                              T∗ = ∞.
