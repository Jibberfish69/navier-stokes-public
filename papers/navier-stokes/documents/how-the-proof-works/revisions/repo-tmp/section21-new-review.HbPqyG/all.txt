The Three-Dimensional Navier–Stokes Regularity
                  Problem
                                  Thomas Birnie

                                 August 24, 2026

                                       Abstract

    We prove global smoothness for the unforced three-dimensional incompressible
Navier–Stokes equations on R3 for every viscosity ν > 0 and every divergence-free
Schwartz initial velocity u0 . The corresponding velocity and pressure remain smooth
for all t ≥ 0.
The proof rules out a finite singular endpoint directly. Let [0, T∗ ) be the maximal
classical lifespan and suppose T∗ < ∞. Repeated time differentiation of the Navier–
Stokes equations, with pressure recovered at each order by Riesz transforms, generates
one pressure-complete mixed jet of the same solution. For each fixed finite temporal
depth N and spatial Sobolev height M , the central estimate controls the corresponding
rectangle of that jet uniformly over every cutoff T < T∗ . The adjacent-row trace
estimate then gives, for every finite r and m,

                               sup ∥∂tr u(t)∥H m (R3 ) < ∞.
                             0≤t<T∗


These finite rectangles agree under restriction. Their compatible intersection preserves
the whole-terminal control at every finite order, and the same trace mechanism gives a
strong terminal limit in every Sobolev space:

                          u(t) −→ u∗     in H m (R3 )       as t ↑ T∗

for every finite m, with one terminal velocity
                                         ∞
                                         \
                                  u∗ ∈         H m (R3 ).
                                         m=0


The differentiated Navier–Stokes equations and the pressure recovery determine the
complete velocity-pressure jet at T∗ . Thus the alleged singular endpoint is a smooth
time slice of the same solution. Smooth local existence restarts the system from u∗ ,
and uniqueness identifies that restart with the original solution. The same solution
then continues beyond T∗ , contradicting maximality. Hence T∗ = ∞.

1    The Regularity Problem
The three-dimensional Navier–Stokes regularity problem asks whether a smooth solution can
lose its smoothness in finite time. The velocity u and pressure p must satisfy

                ∂t u + (u · ∇)u + ∇p = ν∆u,      ∇ · u = 0 on R3 × (0, ∞),
                                                                                                        (1.1)
                                    u(·, 0) = u0 on R3 .

Here u is the R3 -valued velocity, p is the scalar pressure, and ν > 0 is the fixed kinematic
viscosity.
Fefferman’s whole-space formulation assumes that the initial velocity u0 in (1.1) is smooth,
divergence-free, and rapidly decreasing together with all of its spatial derivatives (Fefferman
2000). In standard notation, this means

                            Sσ := S (R3 ; R3 ) ∩ ker(∇·),                u0 ∈ Sσ ,
                                                                                                        (1.2)
         ∀α ∈ N30 ∀K ∈ N0 ∃Cα,K < ∞ ∀x ∈ R3 ,                       |∂xα u0 (x)| ≤ Cα,K (1 + |x|)−K .

Here S (R3 ; R3 ) is the Schwartz space of smooth R3 -valued functions. The symbol α is a
spatial multi-index, and K is the prescribed algebraic decay order. For the fixed datum u0 ,
the constant Cα,K may depend on u0 , α, and K, but not on x.
For finite m ∈ N0 , write
                                Hσm (R3 ) := H m (R3 ; R3 ) ∩ ker(∇·).
Fix K > 3/2. For every |α| ≤ m, (1.2) gives

There are only finitely many multi-indices with |α| ≤ m, so u0 ∈ Hσm (R3 ) for every finite m.
Taking m = 0 gives u0 ∈ L2 (R3 ), so the initial kinetic energy is finite.
Fix this u0 and a viscosity ν > 0. Let (u, p) be the corresponding classical solution on its
maximal smooth interval [0, T∗ ), where T∗ ∈ (0, ∞].
The whole-space claim is that this same solution remains smooth for all time and has uniformly
finite kinetic energy:
                                                              Z

gives ∥u(t)∥2 ≤ ∥u0 ∥2 for every 0 ≤ t < T∗ . The regularity problem is to rule out a finite
endpoint of the smooth history:
                                          T∗ = ∞.

maximal smooth interval. That control does not stop the velocity from changing more
sharply across shorter distances. Viscosity is the part of the equation we expect to stop that
sharpening. It acts alongside the three other pieces of the same motion:

The regularity hope is that viscous smoothing always keeps pace with the sharpening produced
by nonlinear transport. In three dimensions, vortex stretching gives the nonlinear motion a
route to sharpen the flow.

Navier–Stokes Scaling
At a smaller length, viscosity acts more strongly, but nonlinear transport changes with it.
The exact comparison is obtained by rescaling the whole equation. For λ > 1, set

                     uλ (x, t) := λu(λx, λ2 t),          pλ (x, t) := λ2 p(λx, λ2 t).

This produces another solution with the same viscosity at the finer length λ−1 . It compares
the equation across scales; it is not a later state of the material element we just followed.
Every momentum term changes by the same factor:

                                  ∂t uλ (x, t) = λ3 (∂t u)(λx, λ2 t),
                                                                
                           (uλ · ∇)uλ (x, t) = λ3 (u · ∇)u (λx, λ2 t),
                                 ∇pλ (x, t) = λ3 (∇p)(λx, λ2 t),
                                ν∆uλ (x, t) = λ3 ν(∆u)(λx, λ2 t).

A finer scale strengthens viscous diffusion, nonlinear transport, pressure, and local acceleration
together. The equation has given viscosity no relative advantage.

2.2    The temporal Tower
The velocity may be growing or sharpening, and the rate at which it changes has a rate of
change of its own. The same reading continues through every later response. At a fixed
spatial coordinate, the sequence is

                               u,       ∂t u,     ∂t2 u,         ∂t3 u,     ....

Each row is the Eulerian time derivative of the one before it. They are successive responses
of the same field, not separate singularity scenarios. Together they form the temporal Tower.

5      Further Consequences and the Euler Boundary
The finite-time contradiction is complete, but the bounded rectangles carry more information
than continuation alone consumes. They show how the same global history distributes
vorticity across scales, where the classical criteria sit inside the grid, what becomes of the
local singularity picture, and exactly why the argument stops when viscosity is removed.

of the concentration; none supplies the terminal-uniform estimate that would stop it.

B     Derivative and Finite-Record Calculus
Time differentiation opens the temporal rows, and Navier–Stokes identifies their transport,
pressure, and viscosity content. The finite-time lemma then propagates escape through those
existing rows under its stated hypotheses. Spatial differentiation opens the corresponding
columns, and adjacent Sobolev pairs give those rows a common time trace.
