How the Proof Works
For the global three-dimensional Navier–Stokes regularity problem, the fluid obeys
∂t u + (u · ∇)u + ∇p = ν∆u,

∇ · u = 0,

u(0) = u0 ,

with fixed viscosity ν > 0. The question is whether this one fluid can drive its motion into smaller
and smaller scales quickly enough to lose smoothness in finite time, even while its total kinetic energy
stays finite.
That concentration question becomes concrete in a stretching vortex, because the flow itself can
drive the vortex toward a smaller scale. Its vorticity obeys
∂t ω + (u · ∇)ω = (ω · ∇)u + ν∆ω.
When the vorticity aligns with an extensional direction of the strain, (ω · ∇)u amplifies it and
lengthens the vortex along that axis. Incompressibility narrows the cross-section as the vortex
lengthens. The narrowing places the vortex’s transverse velocity variation across a smaller inner
radius. A velocity change that once took place across r0 now takes place across r, so

This is not an a priori estimate proof. It is an intersection-to-estimate proof. Viscosity relates
the velocity’s time derivatives to its spatial Sobolev derivatives. Taken through every finite order,
those two directions meet in a common intersection. At that intersection, H ∞ (R3 ) ,→ C ∞ (R3 ) gives
smoothness, and any familiar finite estimate can be taken from whichever Sobolev height it requires.
The estimates come from the intersection, not the other way around.
