How the Proof Works

The three-dimensional Navier–Stokes regularity problem asks whether a smooth velocity field can become
singular in finite time. The equation carries two opposed effects: nonlinear motion can concentrate
velocity variation into smaller scales, while viscosity diffuses it.

                      ∂t u + (u · ∇)u + ∇p = ν∆u,            ∇ · u = 0,        u(·, 0) = u0 .

To see how the first effect can arise, follow a vortex tube. Surrounding strain pulls it along its axis.
Incompressibility prevents that lengthening from creating volume, so the cross-section contracts and the
rotating core narrows.
Curling the equation shows what the strain does to the vorticity ω = ∇ × u:

                                    ∂t ω + (u · ∇)ω = (ω · ∇)u + ν∆ω.

Where the vortex is aligned with an amplifying direction of the strain,

                                             ω · ((ω · ∇)u) > 0,

the stretching part raises |ω| while the core narrows. Since vorticity is one spatial derivative of velocity,
the velocity changes across less distance and its gradients steepen. The stretching term pushes the
gradients upward as the core narrows; viscosity diffuses them across the core.
Viscosity acts more strongly on a narrower core, but so does the nonlinear term. Scaling measures the
two changes together. For λ > 1, compress the entire solution by setting

                           uλ (x, t) = λu(λx, λ2 t),        pλ (x, t) = λ2 p(λx, λ2 t).

A structure of width r, velocity scale U , and duration τ becomes one of width r/λ, velocity scale λU ,
and duration τ /λ2 . The nonlinear and viscous terms change as

Suppose, for contradiction, that the maximal smooth solution ends at T∗ < ∞.
In order for a singularity to form, velocity has to blow up. In order for that to happen, its acceleration
would also have to blow up, which means its jerk has to blow up, and so on, and so on:

If every finite coupled row can be kept on the whole interval, the ladder runs into smoothness instead.
Take one finite temporal order N and one finite spatial order m. Differentiate only to those orders and
keep the resulting velocity, nonlinear, pressure, and viscous terms in one block. The pressure-complete
recurrence constructs its entries. Every entry is a derivative of one velocity history generated by u0 , so
any coordinate shared with another block is already fixed. The finite mixed estimate controls this block
on the whole interval. In particular,

For every N , both VN and QN lie in H ∞ at T∗ . Smooth local existence restarts the solution from u∗ , and
uniqueness glues that restart to the original velocity history. The solution continues past T∗ , contradicting
maximality. The finite terminal time cannot occur.
This is not an a priori estimate proof. It is an intersection-to-estimate proof. Viscosity relates the
velocity’s time derivatives to its spatial Sobolev derivatives. Taken through every finite order, those two
directions meet in a common intersection. At that intersection, H ∞ (R3 ) ,→ C ∞ (R3 ) gives smoothness,
and the familiar estimates can be taken from whichever finite Sobolev height they require. The estimates
come from the intersection, not the other way around.
