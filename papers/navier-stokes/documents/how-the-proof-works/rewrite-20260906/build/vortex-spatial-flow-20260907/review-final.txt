How the Proof Works

We start with a smooth, incompressible flow of finite energy and keep its viscosity fixed and positive.
The problem is to prove that the flow remains smooth for all time. Its velocity u and pressure p
satisfy the three-dimensional Navier–Stokes equation:
                ∂t u       +        (u· ∇)u          =          −∇p           +        ν∆u ,          ∇· u = 0.
            acceleration       nonlinear transport             pressure           viscous diffusion
The hope is simple: viscosity smooths the fluid faster than nonlinear transport can sharpen it.
Consider the case of a vortex within the fluid. Its vorticity ω = ∇ × u satisfies
                                       ∂t ω + (u · ∇)ω = (ω · ∇)u + ν∆ω.
Strain within the fluid’s flow can naturally stretch a vortex. Incompressibility forces a tube of fluid
particles along its core to narrow as it lengthens.

A narrower core gives viscosity less distance to act across, but the growing velocity can also sharpen
the flow more quickly. To compare these effects at progressively smaller scales, we rescale the flow
by λ > 1:
                                        uλ (x, t) = λu(λx, λ2 t),
                                         pλ (x, t) = λ2 p(λx, λ2 t).
Lengths shrink by λ, velocities grow by λ, and times shrink by λ2 . Every term in the momentum
equation gains a factor λ3 , leaving the rescaled flow governed by the original equation at viscosity ν.
Smaller scales alone give viscosity no advantage over transport.
To measure the concentration, we need to count spatial variation as well as the size of the velocity.
The Sobolev index s sets how strongly we count that variation: integer values count spatial derivatives
in L2 , while fractional values weight frequency ξ by |ξ|s . Velocity contributes λ, differentiation λs ,
and the square root of the volume λ−3/2 , giving
                                  ∥uλ (·, t)∥Ḣ s = λs−1/2 ∥u(·, λ2 t)∥Ḣ s .
Below s = 12 , concentration makes this norm smaller; above s = 12 , it makes it larger. At exactly
s = 12 , the increased variation offsets the shrinking volume. This critical height lets us compare
concentration without the measurement changing simply because we changed scale.

alone cannot exclude this concentration.

In this hypothetical Zeno cascade, infinitely many stages accumulate at a finite time T∗ . There is no
smallest width or final stage of acceleration. The scaling allows this picture; the regularity problem
is whether the fluid can actually carry it out.

up, then jerk, and so on, and so on. These changes in velocity are governed by the Navier–Stokes

The temporal requirements of a singularity have led us into the spatial requirements of smoothness.
If these orders remain controlled together through T∗ , their limiting velocity is smooth enough to
start the flow again.
If a singularity were to form, we would expect the opposite: smoothness at every earlier instant,
but an unbounded fixed Sobolev norm as t approaches T∗ . The temporal responses would become
unbounded too, even while total energy remained finite. Having all finite derivatives before T∗ would
not give us the control needed to carry them through it.
