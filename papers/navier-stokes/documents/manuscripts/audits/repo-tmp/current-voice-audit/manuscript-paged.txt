                  Problem

                                           Abstract


    We prove that for every fixed viscosity ν > 0 and every divergence-free initial datum
u0 ∈ S (R3 ; R3 ), the maximal classical solution of the unforced three-dimensional
incompressible Navier–Stokes equations is global, with velocity and pressure smooth on
R3 × [0, ∞).
   Write [0, T∗ ) for the maximal classical lifespan and suppose that T∗ < ∞. The
central datum-generated estimate is uniform up to T∗ : for every finite N, M ∈ N0 ,
                N                                                         
                      ∥∂tn u∥2L2 (0,T∗ ;H M +1 ) + ∥∂tn+1 u∥2L2 (0,T∗ ;H M −1 ) < ∞.
                X

                n=0

At (N, M ) = (0, 1), the Hilbert-space trace theorem gives u ∈ C([0, T∗ ]; H 1 (R3 )).
Sobolev embedding then gives u ∈ L∞ (0, T∗ ; L3 (R3 )). The Escauriaza–Seregin–Šverák
endpoint criterion makes this bound incompatible with a finite maximal time.
   Under the same finite-time hypothesis, the finite-order velocity and normalized-
pressure families agree on overlaps, and their compatible intersection gives the stronger
endpoint statement: u(t) → u∗ in every H m (R3 ) as t ↑ T∗ , for one divergence-free
u∗ ∈ m<∞ H m (R3 ). At T∗ , the differentiated equations recover every velocity derivative
     T

and the corresponding pressure derivatives under the fixed decaying normalization.
Local well-posedness and uniqueness then restart the same solution from u∗ . The L3
contradiction and the smooth restart both rule out a finite T∗ , so T∗ = ∞. The global
kinetic-energy identity gives ∥u(t)∥2 ≤ ∥u0 ∥2 for every t ≥ 0.

1    The Regularity Problem

    A smooth fluid can only fail globally by reaching a last smooth time. The problem is
to understand whether the same three-dimensional velocity field can keep concentrating
fast enough to create such an endpoint while viscosity is already diffusing the gradients it
produces. That field and its pressure satisfy

                ∂t u + (u · ∇)u + ∇p = ν∆u,              ∇ · u = 0 on R3 × (0, ∞),
                                                                                                 (1.1)
                                       u(·, 0) = u0      on R3 .

Here u is the velocity, p is the pressure required by incompressibility, and the viscosity ν > 0
stays fixed throughout the argument.


   Fefferman’s whole-space problem begins with a smooth divergence-free velocity whose
spatial derivatives decay faster than every algebraic power (Fefferman 2000). Write this
datum class as
                          Sσ := S (R3 ; R3 ) ∩ ker(∇·),            u0 ∈ Sσ ,
                                                                                                 (1.2)
          ∀α ∈ N30 ∀K ∈ N0 ∃Cα,K < ∞ ∀x ∈ R3 ,               |∂xα u0 (x)| ≤ Cα,K (1 + |x|)−K .

The multi-index α chooses a spatial derivative, and K chooses how much algebraic decay to
demand. For the fixed datum, Cα,K may depend on those choices but never on x.


    For finite m ∈ N0 , write

                                Hσm (R3 ) := H m (R3 ; R3 ) ∩ ker(∇·).

This rapid decay already places the datum at every finite Sobolev height. Indeed, fix K > 3/2.
For every |α| ≤ m, (1.2) gives

Only finitely many multi-indices occur below a fixed height m, so u0 ∈ Hσm (R3 ) for every
finite m. The case m = 0 says that the initial kinetic energy is finite.

2     What a Finite Endpoint Would Demand

    Finite energy can survive while a smooth history loses control of its derivatives. In three
dimensions the equation itself supplies the mechanism that could exploit that gap: the same
strain that stretches rotation contracts the structure across it, raising gradients as the width
falls, while viscosity diffuses those gradients. We follow that one competition until it selects
its scale-invariant height. If the height still escapes before a finite last time, the remaining
time then forces the successive responses of the same velocity field.

Navier–Stokes Scaling

   Shrinking ℓ by itself would change the object we are comparing. The Navier–Stokes
equations tie width to velocity, pressure, and time, so a lawful scale change moves all four
together. For λ > 0, define
                     uλ (x, t) := λu(λx, λ2 t),           pλ (x, t) := λ2 p(λx, λ2 t).
Substitution shows that every term acquires the same factor:
                                                                                    
          ∂t uλ (x, t) = λ3 (∂t u)(λx, λ2 t), (uλ · ∇)uλ (x, t) = λ3 (u · ∇)u (λx, λ2 t),
           ∇pλ (x, t) = λ3 (∇p)(λx, λ2 t),               ∆uλ (x, t) = λ3 (∆u)(λx, λ2 t).
Thus the rescaled field obeys the same balance, with
                     x 7→ λ−1 x,       u 7→ λu,           p 7→ λ2 p,       t 7→ λ−2 t.
Moving to a smaller scale amplifies transport and viscosity by the same amount. The equation
therefore gives viscosity no automatic advantage merely because the structure is narrower.
This comparison relates rescaled solutions; it does not yet prove that one fixed fluid actually
travels through those scales.
