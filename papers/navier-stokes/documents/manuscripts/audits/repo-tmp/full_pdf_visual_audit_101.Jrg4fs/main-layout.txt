               Global Smoothness for the Three-Dimensional
                 Incompressible Navier–Stokes Equations
                                             Thomas Birnie

                                              August 2026


                                                 Abstract
         We prove global existence and smoothness for the unforced three-dimensional incompressible
     Navier–Stokes equations on R3 , for every positive viscosity and every divergence-free Schwartz
     initial velocity. Let (u, p) be the pressure-normalized maximal classical solution on [0, T∗ ). The
     main estimate sends the datum to a compatible family of finite mixed rectangles spanning the
     whole interval (0, T∗ ): each chosen temporal row is controlled in two adjacent spatial Sobolev
     spaces, and the differentiated pressure law retains every term generated by transport. If T∗ were
     finite, the adjacent controls would give an endpoint trace at every finite order. Compatibility
     identifies those traces as one H ∞ velocity and pressure jet, while the limiting momentum
     and pressure recurrences make that jet the initial jet of a local restart. Uniqueness joins the
     restart to the original history, contradicting maximality. The proof also keeps the size of its
     conclusion: on each finite horizon it gives finite recursions for all mixed velocity and pressure
     norms, critical-height mass and frequency tails, endpoint moduli, and restart lifespan in terms
     of the datum-generated base rectangle, the required Sobolev sizes of u0 , ν, the horizon, and
     the derivative orders. Replacing the base producer rather than the later intersection steps
     recovers global smoothness with explicit mixed and pressure jets for two-dimensional Euler
     flow, three-dimensional axisymmetric flow without swirl, and forced planar Navier–Stokes flow.
     An exact periodic viscous solution whose critical height initially rises shows that the complete
     compatible family, rather than the sign of one critical readout, carries the continuation argument.

The finite maximal-time problem
1      The whole-space problem and its first possible singular time
The three-dimensional Navier–Stokes regularity problem asks whether a smooth solution can lose
its smoothness in finite time. Fix smooth, divergence-free, finite-energy initial data u0 , fix ν > 0,
and let (u, p) be the corresponding maximal classical solution on [0, T∗ ). We want to prove that T∗
cannot be finite.
    Fefferman’s formulation of the whole-space Millennium Prize Problem takes u0 to be smooth
and divergence-free and imposes a decay estimate on every spatial derivative at every polynomial
order. We write this class as

Here Hσm (R3 ) := {v ∈ H m (R3 ; R3 ) : ∇ · v = 0}. In particular, Sσ (R3 ) ⊂ Hσm (R3 ) for every finite m.
The problem asks for velocity and pressure fields satisfying

                     ∂t u + (u · ∇)u + ∇p = ν∆u,            ∇ · u = 0,      u(0) = u0 ,                (1)

with
                                         u, p ∈ C ∞ (R3 × [0, ∞))
and a kinetic-energy bound uniform in time [1] . Smoothness here includes the endpoint of every finite
interval. It is stronger than being smooth on R3 × (0, T ) for every T < T∗ , because a derivative can
remain finite on every shorter interval and still have no finite trace at T∗ .
    The velocity determines the normalized pressure. Taking the divergence of the momentum
equation and using ∂i ui = 0 gives

This removes the additive function of time left by ∇p in (1). It also fixes an identity that will survive
every later spatial and temporal differentiation. Pressure is never supplied as an independent field
after the velocity has been chosen.

1.1     Smoothness, regularity, and continuation
Three related words will be used throughout the proof, and they should not be asked to do the same
job. A velocity–pressure pair is smooth through a finite time T when

                                         u, p ∈ C ∞ (R3 × [0, T ]).


                                                      5
Equivalently, every mixed spatial and temporal derivative of finite order exists on the open spacetime
region and extends continuously to both temporal faces. Smoothness for all time means smoothness
through every finite T .
    Regularity describes the kind and degree of control presently known. A Sobolev statement such
as u(t) ∈ H m , a mixed-norm statement such as u ∈ Lpt Lqx , and a pointwise statement such as u ∈ C k
are different regularity claims. None may be silently replaced by another without the embedding,
trace, or equation that connects them.
    Suppose a normalized pair (u, p) solves (1) on [0, T ). A continuation through T is a normalized
solution of the same zero-force, fixed-viscosity problem on [0, T + ε), for some ε > 0, which agrees
with (u, p) before T . The Clay problem requires a smooth continuation through every finite time.
A finite quantity is not enough by itself: a required derivative can remain finite on every strict
subinterval and still fail to approach one value at the endpoint.

1.2   Local existence and the maximal history
An H 3 datum generates a unique pressure-normalized strong solution for a time depending only on
ν and its H 3 norm. Every higher integer Sobolev order already present in the datum persists on
that same interval, and a finite maximal lifespan forces the H 3 norm to escape. These are the local
facts used at both ends of the proof [2, 3] ; the proposition states them together, and Appendix C
gives the construction in the form required below.
    The precise statement is the following.

           w ∈ C([t0 , t0 + δ]; Hσm ) ∩ L2 (t0 , t0 + δ; H m+1 ),      wt ∈ L2 (t0 , t0 + δ; H m−1 ).

Compatible local solutions in these strong classes join by uniqueness into one maximal solution on

                                       lim sup ∥w(t)∥H 3 = ∞.

    Suppose instead that ∥w(t)∥H 3 ≤ M for all t < Tmax . The local construction then supplies

the overlap. This extends the maximal solution. The extension uses the actual value of the same
solution at a late time; it does not select a new terminal datum.

2.1    What global weak existence does and does not continue
Leray used the bounds in (6) to construct a global weak solution. For the same datum u0 , there is a

5.5   What can be lost at the endpoint
The slip sheet and the classical continuation criteria leave three possible endpoint failures. A
continuation-grade quantity can become unbounded (Blown); finite one-sided readings can fail to
join (Jump); or the limiting values can cease to satisfy the pressure–viscosity response (Dead ). These
are three obligations of one terminal state—finiteness, compatibility, and the original equation—not
three separate solutions.

Dead. A Dead state has lost lawful response. It is not a zero velocity or a zero derivative: the
zero solution, a stagnation point, and a steady flow can all satisfy Navier–Stokes exactly. At the
base rung the test is
                                      Dt u + ∇p − ν∆u = 0.
If a proposed endpoint assigns a material acceleration that does not equal the limiting pressure–
viscosity response of its surrounding field, that endpoint is Dead at the base rung. The same
mismatch can occur higher when a temporal row no longer answers the transport, diffusion, and
pressure terms obtained by differentiating the equation. Excluding Dead requires more than a
terminal velocity: the endpoint rows and pressure must satisfy the complete differentiated law and
generate the same local restart.

Jump. A Jump is an incompatibility between limiting values of the velocity or of a finite derivative
across a point, interface, or time. The tangential slip sheet (23) is the interface example. At a
terminal time, the same issue can occur when two regular approaches assign different limits to one
spatial or temporal derivative. Both limits may be finite. Separate bounds do not make them
equal. Excluding Jump requires a topology in which the evolving derivative has one continuous
representative and hence one terminal value. Part II will derive that topology from the equation’s
parabolic separation between one time derivative and two spatial derivatives.

Blown. A Blown state has an unbounded velocity, spatial derivative, temporal response, pressure
response, or other finite-depth tower readout. The velocity itself need not diverge. The energy
identity can remain intact while enstrophy or a stronger continuation norm escapes. Excluding
Blown requires a whole-terminal finite bound for every fixed temporal row and every fixed spatial
order needed by smoothness. The constants may depend on those finite orders; no norm uniform
over the infinite tower is required.
    Restart gives these failures their practical significance. At every regular time t0 < T∗ , the value
u(t0 ) is admissible initial data for the local construction in Proposition 1.1. A continuation-grade
bound gives a positive lifespan depending only on that bound and ν. A late enough slice then
generates a solution beyond the candidate endpoint. Regularity is not a list of finite quantities. It is
enough coherent state to let the same equation run again.

Independent uses of the mechanism
23     What must survive when the equation changes
The three-dimensional proof did more than produce a terminal bound. The datum supplied finite
spatial rows of one history; the differentiated equation generated its temporal and pressure rows;
restriction kept the overlaps equal; the intersection produced one endpoint; and uniqueness let that
endpoint restart the evolution. An equation with a different physical producer should preserve the
later steps while changing the estimate that begins them.
    Three independent problems show exactly that. In two-dimensional Euler, viscosity is absent
and scalar vorticity is transported without stretching. That material invariant produces an explicit
double-exponential spatial envelope, after which the mixed rectangles and restart follow. In the
three-dimensional axisymmetric class without swirl, the relative vorticity ω θ /r is the scalar producer
even though the physical vorticity is stretched. In forced two-dimensional Navier–Stokes, vorticity is
no longer conserved; diffusion and the curl of the force instead produce an enstrophy envelope that
pays the complete nonautonomous jet. None of these arguments invokes the main three-dimensional
regularity theorem. The axisymmetric application treats a restricted invariant class, but derives its
global producer directly. The other two begin from their own datum, or from the datum together
with a prescribed force, and then run the same finite-row, endpoint, and restart mechanism. These
are complete proofs of classical global regimes, strengthened here by quantitative mixed and pressure
jets; they are not claims that those classical regimes were previously open. An exact periodic
calculation at the end serves a different purpose: it stress-tests the estimates against a smooth
history whose critical height initially rises.

C.6    Maximal histories and the H 3 alternative
Starting from t0 , join successive local intervals whenever the current solution has an H 3 endpoint
value. Uniqueness identifies the solutions on every overlap, so their union is one pressure-normalized
history on a maximal interval [t0 , Tmax ).
    Assume Tmax < ∞ and
                                           sup ∥w(t)∥H 3 ≤ M.
                                      t0 ≤t<Tmax

The lifespan bound (277) supplies the same positive number

                                    δM := min{1, cν (1 + M )−2 }

whenever the local theorem is restarted from a time t < Tmax . Choose t > Tmax − δM /2 and start the
construction from the actual datum w(t). The new local solution exists past Tmax , and uniqueness
identifies it with the maximal history on their overlap. This contradicts maximality. Hence

                             Tmax < ∞     =⇒       lim sup ∥w(t)∥H 3 = ∞.
                                                   t↑Tmax

    If the initial datum belongs to H m for some m ≥ 3, the persistence argument keeps that order on
every local piece of the maximal history. A bounded H m norm bounds the H 3 norm, so the same late-
slice restart proves the corresponding H m alternative under its datum hypothesis. The construction,
pressure recovery, persistence, and continuation argument together prove Proposition 1.1.
    At the endpoint in Part IV, the compatible intersection supplies u∗ ∈ Hσ∞ , and in particular
u∗ ∈ Hσ3 . The construction above produces a normalized solution on a right-hand interval whose
length depends only on ν and ∥u∗ ∥H 3 . The pressure-complete jet calculation shows that its right
derivatives agree with the already reconstructed left jet. The matching jets make the piecewise pair
classical across T∗ , while local uniqueness preserves the original pair on its old interval.
