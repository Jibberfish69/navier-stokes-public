The Three-Dimensional Navier–Stokes Regularity
                  Problem
                                  Thomas Birnie
                                  August 15, 2026


                                       Abstract
    We prove global existence and smoothness for the unforced three-dimensional
incompressible Navier–Stokes equations on R3 , for every viscosity ν > 0 and every
divergence-free Schwartz initial velocity. Assume that the maximal classical time T∗ is
finite. The energy identity still bounds kinetic energy, but continuation theory forces
both the critical L3 norm and the half-derivative height
                                       1
                                 H(t) = ∥Λ1/2 u(t)∥22
                                       2
to escape every finite bound. A perfectly balanced stretching vortex gives that statement
its physical form: the same fluid would have to complete infinitely many critical-height
first passages before T∗ . Repeated integration in time then forces every fixed temporal
derivative of the velocity to lose the same critical control.
    Differentiating the original equation generates the complete time-and-space derivative
Tower, including the pressure response. Viscosity opens a spatial Sobolev column inside
each temporal row, so the very rows forced toward critical escape also contain stronger
spatial regularity. The whole-terminal rectangle estimate controls every finite piece of
this Tower, and compatibility places all of those pieces in one intersection belonging
to the same fluid history. Its lowest rectangles bound the L3 norm, critical height,
stretching action, and high-frequency tail; its higher rectangles give one common
terminal velocity in H ∞ . Local well-posedness and uniqueness continue that velocity
beyond T∗ , contradicting maximality. Thus T∗ = ∞, the velocity and normalized
pressure are smooth for all time, and kinetic energy remains bounded by its initial
value.

1     The Regularity Problem
1.1    The whole-space regularity problem
Fefferman’s whole-space formulation fixes a positive viscosity, no external force, and a smooth
divergence-free initial velocity on R3 whose derivatives decay faster than every power at
infinity (Fefferman 2000). It asks whether the velocity and pressure generated by every such
datum remain smooth for all time while kinetic energy stays bounded.

Navier–Stokes equations are

                           ∂t u + (u · ∇)u + ∇p = ν∆u,
                                           ∇ · u = 0,                 on R3 .             (1.1.2)
                                          u(·, 0) = u0

   Classical local theory gives a unique smooth solution on a nonzero interval (Fujita and
Kato 1964; Kato 1984). Continue that solution for as long as classical regularity permits,
and denote its maximal interval by [0, T∗ ), where 0 < T∗ ≤ ∞. The problem is to prove

                                                 T∗ = ∞.                                  (1.1.4)

    If T∗ were finite, the solution would still be smooth at every earlier time. Any failure
would be confined to the approach t ↑ T∗ , where a bound strong enough to continue the same
solution would have to escape.

1.2    What a finite last time would require
Assume that the maximal time is finite, and write

                                       T∗ < ∞,      I∗ := (0, T∗ ).

The solution is smooth at every time in I∗ . Any failure is pushed entirely into the terminal
approach, where a norm capable of restarting the same solution has to lose its bound.
   The equation still preserves one decisive low-order quantity all the way to that approach.
Pairing (1.1.2) with u, incompressibility removes transport and pressure, while integration
by parts turns viscosity into dissipation:

one vortex perfectly centred and let the surrounding fluid continue to stretch it.

2     Critical Height and the Derivative Tower
2.1    The perfectly balanced rip-cord vortex
Picture one smooth vortex held exactly on its axis. The surrounding fluid pulls it lengthwise.
Incompressibility makes that extension narrow the cross-section at the same time, so the
spiral around the centre winds tighter and the velocity changes more sharply across the core.
Now remove the ordinary ways that this alignment can be lost: no wobble, no buckling, no
fragmentation, and no off-centre drift. Each narrower state becomes the starting state for
the next stretch. The question is whether the same fluid can complete infinitely many such
tightenings before one finite time.
    This is the perfectly balanced rip-cord vortex. It is not asserted to be an exact special
solution. It is the hardest clean version of the stretching picture: the geometry is kept
favourable to concentration, and the fixed-viscosity equation is left responsible for stopping
it.

actions of one fluid.

levels before one finite last time, with no assumption that H rises monotonically between
crossings. The next question is what that finite-time escape demands from the successive
responses of the velocity itself.

A.4     Silver Standard Proof
The central difficulty is to find an estimate strong enough to survive the limit toward a
hypothetical terminal time, rather than stopping at control of each strict classical subinterval.
Once one digs into that obstruction, it is natural to ask whether smoothness can instead be
proved by excluding every possible nonsmooth continuation of the same fluid.
    Assume you already solved the problem using the Gold standard method.
    That would mean smoothness itself is a property of the Navier-Stokes equations. If
smoothness is a property of the fluid itself, then it must’ve come from the only other
properties in the fluid; velocity, pressure, viscosity, incompressibility, and non-linear transport,
all acting in unison to keep the fluid smooth. Which means any hypothetical “blow-up”
scenario (or any case that breaks smoothness) should not be reachable from those equations,
and any imagined blow-up scenario must not be describing the same fluid from the original
problem statement. If every possible loss of smoothness stops satisfying the original problem,
or stops being about the same fluid, then none of those scenarios are valid Navier-Stokes
counterexamples. If there are no valid counterexamples, then the fluid must be smooth.

                                If Navier–Stokes =⇒ smooth,
                           then not smooth =⇒ not Navier–Stokes.
    The display is a logical equivalence, not a proof of either implication. It becomes a Silver
Standard proof only if every proposed non-smooth outcome is shown, without assuming
smoothness, to be unreachable from the original Navier–Stokes equations. The beauty of
the Silver proof method is that it no longer asks “how is the fluid smooth?”, it only asks
“is it smooth?”; which reveals why the Gold proof method is so hard — Gold requires an
explanation of mechanism (and hence the reason for the podium-titles). So the upside
here, for Silver, is that the burden shifts away from constructing the continuation estimate
directly. The downside is exhaustively handling every non-smooth outcome. Coming up with
a principle that handles every case is the key to simplifying that burden.
