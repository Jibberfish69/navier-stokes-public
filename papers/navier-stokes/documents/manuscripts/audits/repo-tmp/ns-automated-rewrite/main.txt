1    The regularity problem
The three-dimensional Navier–Stokes regularity problem asks whether a smooth solution can lose its
smoothness in finite time. Fix a smooth, divergence-free, finite-energy datum

                                       u0 ∈ Hσ∞ (R3 ) ∩ L2 (R3 ),

fix a viscosity ν > 0, and let (u, p) be the corresponding maximal classical solution on [0, T∗ ):

                     ∂t u + (u · ∇)u + ∇p = ν∆u,           ∇ · u = 0,       u(0) = u0 .              (1)

Here Hσ∞ denotes the divergence-free fields whose Sobolev norms of every finite order are finite. The
endpoint T∗ is maximal: if it is finite, this classical solution cannot be continued through it. We will
prove that T∗ = ∞.
    The pressure is not a second evolution that can be chosen independently of the velocity. Taking
the divergence of (1) and using ∇ · u = 0 gives

                               −∆p = ∂i ∂j (ui uj ),       p = Ri Rj (ui uj ),                       (2)

where Ri = ∂i (−∆)−1/2 is the i-th Riesz transform and the usual decaying whole-space normalization
is understood. Once u(t) is known, this formula determines p(t). The same remains true after time
or space differentiation.
    At the level of kinetic energy, pairing (1) with u eliminates the transport and pressure terms.
After integration in time,
                                            Z t
                              1       2                        1
                                ∥u(t)∥2 + ν     ∥∇u(τ )∥22 dτ = ∥u0 ∥22 .                        (3)
                              2              0                 2

bound for ∇u(t) as t ↑ T∗ , and it gives still less direct control of the higher spatial derivatives. A
finite-time singularity would therefore not need an increasing supply of kinetic energy. It could
instead concentrate the available energy into finer spatial scales over shorter intervals of time. That
is the possibility we now follow.

would have to be carried by its rate of change. A growing acceleration would in turn have to be
carried by its own rate of change, the jerk. Repeating the same reasoning leads naturally to higher
time derivatives. Before differentiating the equation, however, we have to be clear about what
acceleration means for a fluid.

4    The finite-time escalation problem
The critical height can grow while the kinetic energy remains bounded. To see the difference, suppose
a Fourier packet near frequency K satisfies ∥PK u∥22 = A. Its contribution to Es is comparable to

                                                     6
K 2s A. Moving the packet from K to 2K leaves its contribution to E0 unchanged, up to the same
localization constants, but doubles its contribution to E1/2 . In physical space the corresponding
change is toward narrower structures and steeper velocity gradients.
    For such concentration to reach arbitrarily fine scales before a finite time T∗ , an unbounded
sequence of scale changes must fit into the remaining time. The intervals between them must shrink.
This is the Zeno feature of the proposed cascade; it does not depend on whether the concentrating
geometry is a vortex tube, a pulse, or a more complicated transfer between scales.
    The same finite-time compression appears in the temporal derivatives. If a velocity norm becomes
unbounded from a finite starting value, its acceleration cannot remain integrable up to that time. If
the acceleration is the quantity that becomes unbounded, the same statement applies to the jerk.
More generally, for a Banach space X, whenever Vj ∈ W 1,1 (t0 , t; X), the fundamental theorem of
calculus gives                                        Z       t
                             ∥Vj (t)∥X ≤ ∥Vj (t0 )∥X +            ∥Vj+1 (τ )∥X dτ.                  (28)
                                                             t0

Consequently, if ∥Vj (t)∥X becomes unbounded as t ↑ T∗ , then ∥Vj+1 ∥X cannot be integrable on
(t0 , T∗ ). Applied repeatedly, this is the temporal escalation

                                      u,    ut ,   utt ,   uttt ,    ....

The norm may change from one application to the next; the inequality does not assert that every
derivative diverges in one fixed space. Its exact content is that time integrability of row j + 1 gives a
finite terminal trace for row j.

spatial Sobolev energies. Yet concentration toward finer scales is precisely what threatens those

7    Reaching the terminal time
An L2 -in-time bound describes what happens for almost every time before T∗ . By itself it does not
hand us a value at T∗ . To reach the endpoint we need to control how quickly the function can move
as time runs out. There are two versions of that argument. The full spatial column S0 (I∗ ) gives a
direct trace for the velocity. The neighboring rows of each rectangle give the same trace mechanism
for every time derivative.

the gradient component while representing exactly the same pressure-completed nonlinear response

11     The point of the argument
The argument began by asking what would have to lead a finite-time velocity runaway. Acceleration
would have to lead velocity. Jerk would have to lead acceleration. The demand keeps climbing
the temporal tower. Navier–Stokes then answers with the unexpected half of the story: that climb
does not peel spatial control away. Every time row has its own spatial column, and the completed
critical-height identities expose higher and higher Sobolev energies of the original velocity. The two
movements are balanced and opposing rather than complementary and self-feeding.
    The decisive step was not to call some very high row smooth. No individual row is smooth to
every order. Smoothness belongs to the intersection of all finite rows of the same solution. The
rectangle theorem puts that sentence on the entire alleged terminal interval. The trace estimate
then makes time run all the way to T∗ ; compatibility says that every Sobolev view arrives at one
and the same velocity; and the original equation fixes the acceleration, jerk, pressure and every later
derivative at that joining point.
    So the paper can be read in either direction. Forward from Navier–Stokes, the mixed jet produces
the height tower and the completed height tower reveals the Sobolev tower. Backward from the
compatible intersection, the Sobolev tower produces one smooth endpoint, the endpoint reproduces
the pressure-explicit jet, and that jet joins the original history to its continuation. The same fluid is
present in every sentence.
    The fixed chain is now complete:

   u0 −→ {RN,M [u0 ; T∗ ]}N,M −→ I[u0 ] −→ u∗ ∈ H ∞ −→ endpoint jet −→ restart −→ T∗ = ∞.

At each arrow we ask for only the finite derivative order needed there. The spaces are never summed,
and the one datum-generated history is never replaced by independently chosen rows. A proposed
singular time is therefore not an end of the fluid history. It is a smooth joining time inside it.
