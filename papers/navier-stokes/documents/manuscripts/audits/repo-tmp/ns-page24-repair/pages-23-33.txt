Branch Derivation Patterns

The main proof uses one derivation pattern repeatedly. The positive attempt supplies a proposed
continuation mechanism. The failed attempt supplies a selected terminal branch. The class proof
asks which continuation requirement that selected branch loses ﬁrst.
Lemma 3 (Carrier pattern). A terminal branch with zero terminal radius, detached ancestry, ﬁrstcreated unpaid reserve, or terminal carrier birth without a same-ﬂuid charge path fails the carrier
requirement.
Proof. Each listed mechanism removes the positive transported packet needed to carry Q as the
same ﬂuid branch. The carrier requirement is exactly the existence of such a positive transported
packet. The ﬁrst lost requirement is therefore ¬PackQ .
Lemma 4 (Participation pattern). A terminal branch with a surviving carrier and broken pressure
participation, broken viscosity participation, dwell failure, or incompatible local source participation
fails the participation requirement.
Proof. After a carrier survives, the continuation branch still has to solve the same diﬀerentiated
Navier–Stokes equation on that carrier. The listed mechanisms break that same-equation participation while leaving the carrier question already passed. The ﬁrst lost surviving requirement is
therefore PackQ ∧ ¬PartN,Q .
Lemma 5 (Field pattern). A terminal branch with surviving carrier and participation but retained
positive-window blowup, terminal pulse, averaged jump, good-scale failure, or incompatible surviving
traces fails the ﬁeld requirement.
Proof. After carrier and participation survive, membership readout needs one positive scale where
the coherent N -level ﬁeld remains ﬁnite. Each listed mechanism removes that positive-scale readout
while preserving the earlier requirements for the purpose of the case split. The ﬁrst lost remaining
requirement is therefore PackQ ∧ PartN,Q ∧ ∀r > 0 ¬FieldN,r,Q .
Proposition 11 (No hidden branch outside the patterns). Every selected branch handled in the
forward-family analysis is a carrier branch, a participation branch, a ﬁeld branch, a ﬁnite disjunction of those three, or a support theorem awaiting a selected terminal packet.
Proof. A selected branch either lacks a positive transported packet, keeps such a packet and loses
the same Navier–Stokes law through the velocity tower, keeps both and loses positive-scale coherent
ﬁeld readout, or has not yet supplied the selected packet needed for the class test. The ﬁrst three
alternatives are Pack, Part, and Field. The last alternative is support for a later selected packet.
There is no fourth continuation requirement in the working class object.

Proof Attempts And Failures To Prove Smoothness

This appendix records the proof attempts that led into the class-membership proof. The order is
historical and mathematical at the same time. The ﬁrst serious positive program was the four-body
route: control the high scales, bind energy and enstrophy, use that control to build a compact exact
object, and then recover derivative control from a fourth bridge. Later work kept that route alive
while ﬁnding sharper versions of the same problem.
A proof attempt is included here when it tried to control smoothness, remove a possible terminal
defect, or translate a public critical signal into the native Navier–Stokes witness language. For each
23

route the useful question is plain: what did it try to pay, what calculation did it use, and where
did the payment fail?

B.1

The Four-Body Circuit

The source-grounded smoothness program was a four-part circuit. It tried to move one packet of
control around a loop:
Scl −→ Qcl ,

(Scl , Qcl ) −→ Ccl ,

Ccl −→ Gcl ,

Gcl −→ Scl .

Here Scl is the scale-barrier body, Qcl is the energy/enstrophy body, Ccl is the compactness body,
and Gcl is the gradient or curvature body.
The attraction of the route is easy to see. A smoothness proof needs a way to keep the derivative
stack ﬁnite. A scale barrier would stop energy from escaping into arbitrarily high frequencies. A
bounded Q(t) would keep the energy/enstrophy level from feeding the cascade. Compactness would
turn a controlled sequence into an exact Navier–Stokes object. The fourth body would upgrade
that object back into derivative control, closing the loop.
The circuit fails as a completed proof because none of the four bodies can carry the whole burden
alone. The scale barrier needs a nonlinear transfer estimate. The Q(t) body needs the nonlinear
source term to be paid. Compactness needs scale and energy control before it can produce a useful
limit. The curvature body in the recovered source stack is geometric; a same-surface Euclidean or
torus gradient theorem remains a separate theorem.
The ﬁrst durable lesson is therefore this: the proof has to say exactly how nonlinear transfer, energy/enstrophy, compactness, and gradient recovery pay one another without changing the
problem.

B.10

Signed Descent

The signed descent route tries to use cancellation before taking absolute values. The lifted commutator is rewritten as logarithmic scale descent, then telescoped, and only after that pushed to
a positive residual:
signed lifted bulk −→ boundary packet + mesoscopic residual.
The mathematical gain is real. The bulk is no longer the live burden. The route has converted
the bulk into edge objects that are smaller and more structured.
The failure is that the boundary packet and mesoscopic residual still require a terminal payment.
Once the telescoping stops, the proof still has to show that the edge packet carries no missing source
into the terminal window.

B.11

Volterra And Lower-Triangular Memory

The Volterra route recognizes the one-sided tail as a memory operator. It then rewrites the scale
interaction as a lower-triangular operator. The persistent mode is no longer the full preﬁx. The
surviving quotient is ﬁnite-dimensional at the descriptive level:
one-sided preﬁx −→ weighted quotient mode −→ (M0 , M1 ).
This route is valuable because it says that much of the apparent inﬁnite tail is bookkeeping.
After quotienting, only a small family of moments remains visible.
The failure is quantitative. A quotient description still lacks contraction. The proof still needs
a theorem saying that the moment pair vanishes, loses the required terminal strength, or forces a
Pack, Part, or Field failure after CM entry.

B.14

Source-Wall Deletion Cycle

Once the Q(t) body exposed the nonlinear source, the repo repeatedly tried to delete that source.
The pattern was stable:
ﬁnd source pulse or reserve −→ try to charge it −→ ﬁnd a sharper survivor.
The BASAC, pure-pressure, transported-boundary, angular-saturation, quantized-parent, Zeno,
and pressure-sustain notes belong to this family. They try to prove that any source residue entering
the terminal window brings an incoming payment, a compact ancestry, a boundary ﬂux, a pressure
charge, or a rigid Zeno class that can be exhausted.
The family is large because each proposed payment opens its own payment question. A source
pulse can be assigned to a parent; the parent can require an earlier parent; the ancestry can become
terminally compressed; the pressure readout can hide in an angular channel; the transported
boundary can be too thin; the Zeno class can carry residue without the visible ﬂux the previous
argument wanted.
The failure is a repeated payment gap. The direct positive estimate keeps ﬁnding a source
bill that the known energy law has left unpaid. That is why the source-wall family eventually
becomes useful to the CM route: a source object with no same-solution continuation payment may
be evidence that the alleged terminal branch has left the class.
