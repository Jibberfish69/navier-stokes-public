% ============================================================
% 2.1 Vortex Stretching
% ============================================================

The momentum equation has four pieces:
\[
  \underbrace{\partial_t u}_{\text{local acceleration}}
  +
  \underbrace{(u\cdot\nabla)u}_{\text{nonlinear transport}}
  =
  \underbrace{-\nabla p}_{\text{pressure}}
  +
  \underbrace{\nu\Delta u}_{\text{viscous diffusion}}.
\]
The central regularity problem lies in the competition between nonlinear transport and viscosity.
The hope is simple: viscosity smooths the fluid faster than nonlinear transport can sharpen it.
If that remains true at every scale, the velocity stays smooth.

\subsection{Vortex Stretching}\label{sub:ThePerfectlyStretchingVortex}

When the surrounding strain pulls a material vortex segment along its own axis, that same piece of fluid lengthens. If \(e\) is the stretching direction and \(L(t)\) is the segment length, the symmetric strain records that extension through
\[
  S:=\frac12\bigl(\nabla u+(\nabla u)^{\mathsf T}\bigr),
  \qquad
  Se=\sigma(t)e,
  \qquad
  \sigma(t)>0,
  \qquad
  \frac{\dot L(t)}{L(t)}
  =
  e\cdot Se
  =
  \sigma(t).
\]
Because the fluid is incompressible, that gain in length has to be paid for across the core. With material volume \(\mathcal V\) fixed, define the effective transverse area by \(A(t):=\mathcal V/L(t)\) and the effective radius by \(r_{\mathrm{eff}}(t):=\sqrt{A(t)/\pi}\). Then
\[
  \nabla\cdot u=0,
  \qquad
  \frac{d}{dt}(A L)=0,
  \qquad
  \frac{\dot A}{A}=-\sigma(t),
  \qquad
  \frac{\dot r_{\mathrm{eff}}}{r_{\mathrm{eff}}}
  =
  -\frac{\sigma(t)}{2}.
\]
With \(\omega:=\nabla\times u\), the vorticity along the same material trajectory satisfies
\[
  \frac{D\omega}{Dt}
  =
  S\omega+\nu\Delta\omega,
  \qquad
  \frac{D}{Dt}
  :=
  \partial_t+u\cdot\nabla,
\]
and at a material point where \(\omega=|\omega|e\),
\[
  S\omega
  =
  \sigma(t)\omega,
  \qquad
  \frac12\frac{D|\omega|^2}{Dt}
  =
  \sigma(t)|\omega|^2
  +
  \nu\,\omega\cdot\Delta\omega.
\]
Along any part of the trajectory where this full right-hand side stays positive, the aligned vorticity strengthens while the effective transverse radius contracts. The energy identity proved in \Cref{prop:ClassicalKineticEnergyIdentity} and the antisymmetric part of \(\nabla u\) give
\[
  \norm{u(t)}_2
  \leq
  \norm{u_0}_2
  <\infty,
  \qquad
  \left|\frac12\bigl(\nabla u-(\nabla u)^{\mathsf T}\bigr)\right|_{\mathrm F}
  =
  \frac{|\omega|}{\sqrt2}
  \leq
  |\nabla u|_{\mathrm F}.
\]
Finite kinetic energy can persist while the rotational part of the gradient grows as the segment narrows. The gradients rise.

\divider{\NavierStokes{} Scaling}

At a time \(t_0<T_*\), write \(\ell:=r_{\mathrm{eff}}(t_0)\). To test whether the smaller width gives viscosity any relative gain, compare the original solution with the exact \NavierStokes{} rescaling
\[
  u_\lambda(x,t)
  :=
  \lambda u(\lambda x,\lambda^2t),
  \qquad
  p_\lambda(x,t)
  :=
  \lambda^2p(\lambda x,\lambda^2t).
\]
At time \(\lambda^{-2}t_0\), the corresponding segment in \(u_\lambda\) has effective radius \(\lambda^{-1}\ell\). This is another solution at another scale, not a later state of the original segment. Its momentum terms transform by
\[
\begin{aligned}
  \partial_tu_\lambda(x,t)
  &=
  \lambda^3(\partial_tu)(\lambda x,\lambda^2t),
  \\
  (u_\lambda\cdot\nabla)u_\lambda(x,t)
  &=
  \lambda^3\bigl((u\cdot\nabla)u\bigr)(\lambda x,\lambda^2t),
  \\
  \nabla p_\lambda(x,t)
  &=
  \lambda^3(\nabla p)(\lambda x,\lambda^2t),
  \\
  \nu\Delta u_\lambda(x,t)
  &=
  \lambda^3\nu(\Delta u)(\lambda x,\lambda^2t).
\end{aligned}
\]
All four terms scale by the same \(\lambda^3\). Passing to finer width does not move viscosity ahead of nonlinear transport.

\divider{Critical Height}

The equation keeps that balance across the scaled family, but the global Sobolev measurements of the field do not move together. Let \(\Lambda:=(-\Delta)^{1/2}\). Since
\[
  \widehat{u_\lambda}(\xi,t)
  =
  \lambda^{-2}\widehat u(\lambda^{-1}\xi,\lambda^2t),
\]
a change of variables gives
\[
  \norm{u_\lambda(t)}_{\dot H^s}^2
  =
  \lambda^{2s-1}
  \norm{u(\lambda^2t)}_{\dot H^s}^2.
\]
At \(s=0\), kinetic energy loses one power of \(\lambda\); at \(s=1\), the first-derivative norm gains one. The exponent vanishes at \(s=\tfrac12\). Set
\[
  H(t)
  :=
  \frac12\norm{u(t)}_{\dot H^{1/2}}^2
  =
  \frac12\norm{\Lambda^{1/2}u(t)}_2^2.
\]
Writing \(H_\lambda\) for the same quantity evaluated on \(u_\lambda\),
\[
  \norm{u_\lambda(t)}_2^2
  =
  \lambda^{-1}\norm{u(\lambda^2t)}_2^2,
  \qquad
  H_\lambda(t)=H(\lambda^2t),
  \qquad
  \norm{\nabla u_\lambda(t)}_2^2
  =
  \lambda\norm{\nabla u(\lambda^2t)}_2^2.
\]
The rescaled field carries less kinetic energy and a larger first-derivative norm, while \(H\) stays fixed across the scaled family. This is the scale-invariant Sobolev level of the problem.

\divider{Nonlinear Production versus Viscous Dissipation}

Its change has to be read along the original solution itself. At that level, nonlinear transport contributes the signed production
\[
  \mathcal P_{1/2}[u](t)
  :=
  -\operatorname{Re}
  \pair
    {\Lambda^{1/2}u(t)}
    {\Lambda^{1/2}\bigl((u(t)\cdot\nabla)u(t)\bigr)}_{L^2}.
\]
The pressure pairing vanishes because \(\nabla\cdot u=0\), and the Laplacian contributes \(-\nu\norm{\Lambda^{3/2}u}_2^2\). The critical height obeys
\[
  H'(t)
  +
  \nu\norm{\Lambda^{3/2}u(t)}_2^2
  =
  \mathcal P_{1/2}[u](t).
\]
So \(H\) rises only where the signed nonlinear production exceeds viscous dissipation. Under the same rescaling,
\[
  \mathcal P_{1/2}[u_\lambda](t)
  =
  \lambda^2\mathcal P_{1/2}[u](\lambda^2t),
  \qquad
  \nu\norm{\Lambda^{3/2}u_\lambda(t)}_2^2
  =
  \lambda^2\nu\norm{\Lambda^{3/2}u(\lambda^2t)}_2^2.
\]
In matched scaled solutions, production and dissipation transform by the same \(\lambda^2\) factor. The unresolved competition survives unchanged, and that common homogeneity pairs with the coordinate-time contraction \(t\mapsto\lambda^{-2}t\).

\divider{The Heat Clock}

To give that contraction a viscous meaning, add a spectral-localization hypothesis at comparison width \(r\). For the heat equation \(v_t=\nu\Delta v\),
\[
  \widehat v(\xi,t+\delta t)
  =
  e^{-\nu|\xi|^2\delta t}\widehat v(\xi,t).
\]
If the relevant Fourier content lies in a band \(|\xi|\simeq r^{-1}\), then heat flow produces order-one damping when
\[
  \nu|\xi|^2\delta t\simeq1,
\]
so the conditional viscous comparison time is
\[
  \tau_\nu(r)
  :=
  \frac{r^2}{\nu}.
\]
Here \(r\) is a comparison width; identifying it with the earlier material effective radius requires an additional localization estimate. Narrowing that comparison width by \(\lambda^{-1}\) gives
\[
  \tau_\nu(\lambda^{-1}r)
  =
  \lambda^{-2}\tau_\nu(r).
\]
The same square contraction now appears as a shortened viscous comparison clock.

\divider{The Zeno Cascade}

Iterating the halving gives
\[
  r_n:=2^{-n}r_0,
  \qquad
  \tau_n:=\frac{r_n^2}{\nu},
\]
and hence
\[
  \tau_n
  =
  \frac{r_0^2}{\nu}4^{-n},
  \qquad
  \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\tau_n
  =
  \frac{4r_0^2}{3\nu}
  <
  \infty.
\]
The clocks are summable. For them to describe a candidate singular history, one actual \NavierStokes{} solution would have to cross an ordered sequence of widths
\[
  r_0>r_1>r_2>\cdots\longrightarrow0
\]
at times
\[
  t_0<t_1<t_2<\cdots\uparrow T_*<\infty,
  \qquad
  t_{n+1}-t_n\asymp\tau_n,
\]
with comparison constants independent of \(n\). Under that one-solution hypothesis, the remaining time satisfies
\[
  T_*-t_n
  \asymp
  \sum_{k=n}^{\infty}\tau_k
  =
  \frac{4r_n^2}{3\nu}.
\]
At width \(r_n\), the next transition and the full remaining tail are both of order \(r_n^2/\nu\). A candidate cascade would then force one velocity field to supply successive changes of scale on intervals collapsing at exactly the rate set by the widths themselves.
