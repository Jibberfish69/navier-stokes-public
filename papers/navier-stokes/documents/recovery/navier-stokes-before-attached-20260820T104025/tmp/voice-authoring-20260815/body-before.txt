                                         Abstract
    Fefferman’s assertion (A) asks whether every smooth, rapidly decaying, divergence-
free initial velocity u0 on R3 generates a global smooth finite-energy Navier–Stokes
solution for each ν > 0. We prove that it does.
    Assume instead that one such fluid has a finite last time T∗ . Its kinetic energy
remains bounded, while the scale-critical L3 norm has to escape. A perfectly balanced
rip-cord vortex makes that concentration threat visible: coherent axial stretching
narrows the core and presses velocity variation across shorter distances. Navier–Stokes
scaling selects H(t) = 12 ∥Λ1/2 u(t)∥22 , and the L3 escape forces H through infinitely
many dyadic first-passage levels before T∗ . Those accumulating crossings are the Zeno
geometry of the alleged singularity.
    The same L3 escape forces every fixed Eulerian response Vn = ∂tn u to lose uniform
  3
L control: if one higher response stayed bounded, repeated integration would bound
the rows below it. Differentiating the equation shows that these temporal rows already
contain viscosity, transport, and pressure derivatives. Separately, the n-th critical-height
derivative is an exact quadratic contraction of V0 , . . . , Vn ; after every transfer term is
retained, its completed scalar row is En+1/2 . The adjacent velocity rows at finite spatial
depths form the temporal–spatial rectangles used in the proof.
    The decisive whole-terminal estimate controls every chosen finite temporal–spatial
rectangle by the initial datum, uniformly as the cutoff approaches T∗ . Intersecting those
rectangles places the one velocity history in every finite mixed Sobolev class. A low
rectangle already gives a finite roof for critical height, only finitely many Zeno levels,
positive separation of persistent material labels, and a uniformly vanishing critical tail
for a recruited core. The full intersection gives one smooth terminal velocity, which
starts the next classical interval. This contradicts the existence of a finite last time.
Hence T∗ = ∞, and the velocity and normalized pressure are smooth for all time.

1.1    Fefferman’s criterion
Fefferman’s assertion (A) asks for a global smooth solution of the three-dimensional incom-
pressible Navier–Stokes equations on R3 . Take any viscosity ν > 0, set the external force equal
to zero, and prescribe a smooth divergence-free initial velocity u0 whose spatial derivatives
decay faster than any power of |x|. The required conclusion is the existence of a velocity
u(x, t) and pressure p(x, t), defined for every t ≥ 0, which satisfy

                                 ∂t u + (u · ∇)u + ∇p = ν∆u,
                                                 ∇ · u = 0,                              (1.1.1)
                                                u(·, 0) = u0 ,

together with                                              Z
                   u, p ∈ C ∞ R3 × [0, ∞) ,            sup        |u(x, t)|2 dx < ∞.     (1.1.2)
                                                       t≥0   R3

This is assertion (A) in the official problem description (Fefferman 2000). It is the assertion
proved here.
    The equation describes one fluid undergoing four simultaneous effects. The velocity
transports itself through (u · ∇)u. Incompressibility makes every local deformation part
of a volume-preserving motion of the surrounding field. Pressure enforces that constraint
instantaneously, and viscosity diffuses the spatial variation created by transport. None of
these effects belongs to a separate fluid or a separate stage of the motion. The regularity
problem asks what their coupled action can do to the same velocity field over an unlimited
time.
    Pressure carries this coupling explicitly. Decay fixes its additive ambiguity, and taking
the divergence of the momentum equation gives

                           −∆p = ∂i ∂j (ui uj ),        p = Ri Rj (ui uj ),              (1.1.3)

where Rj = ∂j (−∆)−1/2 is the j-th Riesz transform. The pressure at time t is determined by
the velocity at that same time. It is neither an independent initial datum nor a force that
can be adjusted when the motion becomes difficult.
    Classical local theory starts this motion from every admissible u0 and determines it
uniquely for a nonzero time (Fujita and Kato 1964; Kato 1984). Continuing that same
solution for as long as the classical theory permits produces a maximal history

                      (u, p) : [0, T∗ ) × R3 −→ R3 × R,             0 < T∗ ≤ ∞.

Every time and space derivative used below belongs to this u, and every pressure derivative
belongs to the pressure reconstructed by (1.1.3). Fefferman’s criterion is now the single
statement
                           T∗ = ∞      for every admissible u0 .                    (1.1.4)

lives in that narrowing gap between what energy preserves and what a late restart requires.

1.2      What a finite last time would mean
Assume for contradiction that T∗ < ∞, and stay with the maximal history just constructed.
There is no singular frame at any time t < T∗ . For every sequence tj ↑ T∗ , each velocity
u(·, tj ) is a smooth divergence-free field and can be used as fresh initial data for the same
local theory. Finite maximality says that the lifespan produced by these later restarts shrinks
so severely that no restart crosses T∗ .
    The energy norm cannot supply this escape. Equation (1.1.5) keeps ∥u(t)∥2 below ∥u0 ∥2
all the way to the alleged endpoint, while the dissipative term pays only the time integral
of ∥∇u(t)∥22 . The same history can retain finite kinetic energy while its velocity variation is
pressed across shorter spatial distances during shorter intervals. At every preterminal time
the variation is still finite; along the terminal sequence it may grow without a uniform roof.
    The cleanest version of that threat is a vortex that never loses the organization driving
its own concentration. Holding the vortex exactly on its axis, and removing wobble, buckling,
fragmentation, and off-centre drift, leaves transport, incompressibility, pressure, and viscosity
themselves responsible for deciding whether the tightening can continue.

2.1    The perfectly balanced rip-cord vortex
Picture one smooth vortex held exactly on its axis. The surrounding motion of the same
fluid pulls it lengthwise. Incompressibility does not allow that extension to occur by itself:
while the tube lengthens, its cross-section is drawn inward, the spiral around the center is
pressed into a narrower region, and the velocity variation across the core is forced across a
shorter distance. The vortex is still smooth after this first stretch, but its narrower state is
now the starting state for the next one.
    Now remove the ordinary ways in which that organization might be lost. Do not let the
tube wobble away from its axis, buckle, fragment, or shed the symmetry that keeps the pull
centered. Let each tightening remain coherent enough to feed the next tightening. If that
process could repeat at finer and finer scales while the elapsed times shrink, the same fluid
would be asked to complete infinitely many smooth tightenings before one finite time.
    This is the perfectly balanced rip-cord vortex. It is a clean limiting concentration scene,
not a proposed exact solution. An imbalanced vortex may gyrate, mix, and waste the
alignment that drives the narrowing; those effects may help a particular flow, but a regularity
proof cannot depend on them. The balanced scene removes those geometric escape routes
and leaves the equation itself responsible for stopping the climb.
    The vorticity equation contains the competition governing this scene. With

Along any sequence on which the critical L3 norm escapes, the velocity amplitude must escape
somewhere in space. The conclusion is global, and the distinction matters: the centered
vortex explains the concentration threat, but the continuation theorem does not nominate
that center as the blow-up point.
    There are two separate ways in which the visible core can keep narrowing. In the first, the
same material labels remain inside the core through every stretch. The radius then measures
an actual separation between particles of the original fluid. Driving that radius to zero in
finite time requires those labels to accumulate enough deformation along their trajectories to
erase a positive initial distance.
    In the second, the visible core keeps its shape by recruiting new material while earlier
labels leave it. No fixed pair of particles has to coalesce, so a trajectory argument cannot
describe the danger. The danger belongs to the whole velocity field: the narrowing pattern
would have to retain its strength while moving into finer spatial scales. These are the two
readings carried by the same narrowing scene, and Section 4.3 closes them separately.
    To distinguish genuine growth from a change of scale, the narrowing must be measured
by a quantity invariant under Navier–Stokes rescaling.
