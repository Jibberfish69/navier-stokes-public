2.1    Vortex Stretching
The momentum equation has four pieces:

The central regularity question is whether three-dimensional nonlinear transport—through
vortex stretching and the transfer of activity toward smaller scales—can generate gradients
faster than viscosity dissipates them.
The hope is simple. Viscosity smooths the fluid faster than nonlinear transport can sharpen
it. If that remains true at every scale, the velocity stays smooth.


Vortex Stretching
Vortex stretching is the three-dimensional motion that can keep this sharpening alive. Vorticity
and the symmetric strain are

The vector ω measures local rotation, while S measures extension and contraction in the
surrounding velocity field. In three dimensions, the surrounding velocity can extend a rotating
structure along its axis. If ω aligns with a positive extensional direction of S, then

                                                     ω · Sω > 0,

so the strain amplifies the vorticity.
Incompressibility ties that extension to a contraction:

                                                 tr S = ∇ · u = 0.

Extension in one direction must be accompanied by a net contraction across the transverse
cross-section. Suppose the rotating structure has transverse width ℓ and the velocity changes
by ∆U across that width. The corresponding gradient is roughly

If ℓ shrinks while ∆U does not shrink equally fast, the gradient grows. Thinning by itself
guarantees nothing; the velocity change across the shrinking width has to persist.

The vorticity equation
Taking the curl removes pressure and makes the competition exact:

                              ∇ × ∇p = 0,
                       ∂t ω + (u · ∇)ω = (ω · ∇)u + ν∆ω = Sω + ν∆ω.

Here Sω is vortex stretching and ν∆ω is viscous diffusion.

This identity does not decide which effect wins. It identifies what still has to be compared.
If aligned extension carries the concentration into a smaller width, vortex stretching and
viscous diffusion both act on that same smaller structure. The unresolved issue is whether
the move to smaller scale gives viscosity an advantage over stretching.


Navier–Stokes Scaling
That comparison cannot be made by shrinking the width while holding the rest of the flow
fixed. The Navier–Stokes equations tie spatial width to velocity amplitude, pressure, and
time, so all four quantities have to be rescaled together. For λ > 0, define

                     uλ (x, t) := λu(λx, λ2 t),       pλ (x, t) := λ2 p(λx, λ2 t).

Local acceleration, nonlinear transport, pressure, and viscosity all scale together. The
corresponding scales are
                     x 7→ λ−1 x,          u 7→ λu,         p 7→ λ2 p,         t 7→ λ−2 t.
Smaller scale gives viscosity no automatic advantage over nonlinear transport: under the full
rescaling, the two terms retain the same relative strength. This scaling compares solutions at
different scales. A cascade inside one fixed solution is an additional dynamical claim.
