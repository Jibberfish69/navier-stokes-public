The Three-Dimensional Navier–Stokes Regularity
                  Problem
                                  Thomas Birnie
                                 August 18, 2026


                                       Abstract
    We prove that, for every viscosity ν > 0 and every divergence-free Schwartz initial
velocity u0 on R3 , the maximal classical solution of the unforced three-dimensional
incompressible Navier–Stokes equations is global, with velocity and pressure smooth for
all time. If its lifespan ended at a finite T∗ , every fixed finite block of temporal and
spatial derivatives of that same solution would remain uniformly controlled over all
cutoffs T < T∗ . At each spatial order, the equation also controls the time derivative, so
the same solution approaches a single terminal velocity u∗ ∈ H ∞ as t ↑ T∗ . Local well-
posedness continues the original solution from u∗ beyond T∗ , contradicting maximality.
The global solution obeys the energy identity, which bounds kinetic energy by its initial
value.

Smoothness in (1.3) means that every spatial and temporal derivative of both fields exists
and is continuous for all t ≥ 0. Regularity is the assertion that this smoothness persists
throughout the evolution. A continuation past a finite time T extends the original velocity
to [0, T + ε), for some ε > 0, while solving the same initial-value problem there. Finite-time
loss of regularity occurs when this smooth extension fails at some finite T .

2     Critical Height and the Derivative Tower
2.1    The balance at critical height
Viscosity never stops smoothing the velocity. The difficulty is that the fluid can keep making
its velocity differences steeper on the same scales where that smoothing becomes stronger.
The regularity problem lives in this competition. If shrinking the active scale eventually
gave viscosity an advantage, then concentration would carry its own obstruction. If the two
mechanisms strengthen together, going to a smaller scale settles nothing.
    In three dimensions, vortex stretching puts this balance into an actual motion. Take a
material tube through a region where the vorticity is concentrated. The tube follows fluid
particles; because viscosity diffuses vorticity across particle labels, it should not be identified
with a material vortex core for later times. Incompressibility preserves the volume of that
material tube, so a segment that lengthens must lose average transverse cross-sectional area.
The velocity then turns across a thinner region and its transverse gradients can become
steeper. Viscosity sees those steeper gradients immediately. At the same time, positive
extensional strain aligned with the vorticity amplifies the vorticity carried through the region.
    None of this says that the alignment will survive, that the strain will keep its sign, or
that one contraction will reproduce another. Those are dynamical questions. It identifies the
dangerous case: enough aligned extension persists for the vortex to sharpen while viscosity is
already acting on the same shrinking structure.

and integration by parts below is made on that interval.

2.3    Velocity, acceleration, and the Tower
A shorter clock creates a larger response only if the motion still makes a definite change.
If the velocity completes the same nontrivial change in half the time, its rate of change
must double somewhere on that interval. Along a fluid particle, this rate is acceleration. If
that acceleration completes a definite change on the next shorter clock, its material rate of
change—material jerk—has to grow in turn. Repeating the demand begins a response Tower
through velocity, acceleration, jerk, and every later response that the motion continues to
activate.
    The definite change carries as much weight as the shorter interval. A quantity that
has stopped changing can sit on an arbitrarily short interval with a small derivative. The
Zeno clocks supply shrinking denominators, and the dynamics would still have to supply a
nontrivial increment on every rung.
