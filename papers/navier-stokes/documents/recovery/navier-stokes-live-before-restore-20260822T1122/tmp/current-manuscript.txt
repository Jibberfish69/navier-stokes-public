The Three-Dimensional Navier–Stokes Regularity
                  Problem
                                  Thomas Birnie
                                 August 18, 2026


                                      Abstract
    We prove that, for every viscosity ν > 0 and every divergence-free Schwartz initial
velocity u0 on R3 , the corresponding maximal classical solution of the unforced three-
dimensional incompressible Navier–Stokes equations is global, with velocity and pressure
smooth for all time, and satisfies the energy identity. Assume instead that its maximal
time T∗ is finite. For every finite temporal order r and spatial order m, the argument
bounds ∂tr u in H m (R3 ) uniformly up to T∗ . Since these are derivatives of the same
solution, the bounds give a single smooth terminal velocity, and the equation determines
every velocity and pressure derivative there. Smooth local existence and uniqueness
then continue that same solution beyond T∗ , contradicting maximality.

1     The Regularity Problem
The three-dimensional Navier–Stokes regularity problem asks whether a smooth solution can
lose its smoothness in finite time. The velocity u and pressure p must satisfy

                 ∂t u + (u · ∇)u + ∇p = ν∆u,      ∇ · u = 0 on R3 × (0, ∞),
                                                                                              (1.1)
                                     u(·, 0) = u0 on R3 .

Here u is the R3 -valued velocity, p is the scalar pressure, and ν > 0 is the fixed kinematic
viscosity.
    Fefferman’s whole-space formulation assumes a smooth, divergence-free initial velocity
whose derivatives decay faster than every algebraic rate (Fefferman 2000). We denote this
class by Sσ :

                                                                               

The constant Cα,K may depend on a, the multi-index α, and the decay order K, while
remaining independent of x. Choosing K > 3/2 for the derivatives up to any fixed order gives

          Hσm (R3 ) := {v ∈ H m (R3 ; R3 ) : ∇ · v = 0},          Sσ ⊂ Hσm (R3 ) (m ∈ N0 ).

In particular, u0 begins smooth, divergence-free, rapidly decreasing, and with finite kinetic
energy. Any later loss of regularity has to be generated by the Navier–Stokes evolution.
    Fix u0 ∈ Sσ and ν > 0. The equation determines p up to an additive function of time.
Proposition A.1.1 fixes this freedom by requiring p(·, t) ∈ H 3 (R3 ) and gives a unique maximal
classical solution (u, p) on R3 × [0, T∗ ), where T∗ ∈ (0, ∞]. For every T < T∗ , the pair is
smooth on R3 × [0, T ] and solves (1.1) with the same datum u0 . A continuation through a
finite T∗ would extend this solution to [0, T∗ + ε) for some ε > 0 and agree with it throughout
[0, T∗ ). The continuation alternative in Proposition A.1.1 states that

                             T∗ < ∞      =⇒       lim sup ∥u(t)∥H 3 = ∞.
                                                   t↑T∗


A finite endpoint is accompanied by unbounded H 3 velocity along the terminal approach.
    The whole-space assertion asks that this maximal solution exist for all time and satisfy

                    u, p ∈ C       R × [0, ∞) ,        sup        |u(x, t)|2 dx < ∞.          (1.3)

(1.3) is controlled throughout the maximal lifespan. Local theory supplies smoothness on
each compact subinterval of [0, T∗ ). The regularity problem is the assertion that this lifespan
has no finite endpoint:
                                           T∗ = ∞.

2     Critical Height and the Derivative Tower
Finite energy does not prevent the velocity from sharpening in one part of the fluid. A
nontrivial change can be carried through less and less distance while the total L2 -size remains
bounded. That shrinking distance is the concentration a singularity would have to exploit.
In three dimensions, vortex stretching is the motion that can drive it.

2.1    Vortex Stretching
The surrounding strain pulls the rotating region along its axis, and the region lengthens.
The condition ∇ · u = 0 preserves the volume of every material tube. If that tube remains
tube-like while it stretches, its average transverse area has to decrease. The contraction need
not be the same in both transverse directions.
    The vorticity itself is not frozen inside that material tube. Viscosity continues to diffuse
it across the moving particle labels. The dangerous motion is the coupled one in which strain
keeps amplifying the rotation while the vorticity core remains concentrated across smaller
transverse widths. If the velocity difference across that core does not flatten at the same rate,
the same variation is being carried through less distance and the spatial gradient grows. This
is the stretching vortex we need to follow.
    Write

2.2     The Heat Clock and the Zeno Cascade

heat equation. If vt = ν∆v and the Fourier mass of v lies near |ξ| ≃ r−1 , then

    That is a comparison clock. The heat calculation does not create a smaller width in the

2.3    The temporal Tower
A singularity would not appear beside the velocity field as a separate event. It would be
a loss of regularity taking shape inside that field as the terminal time is approached. The
velocity may be growing or sharpening, and the rate at which it changes has a rate of change
of its own. The same reading continues through every later response. At a fixed spatial
coordinate, the sequence is

                             u,     ∂t u,     ∂t2 u,    ∂t3 u,    ....

Each row is the Eulerian time derivative of the one before it. They are successive responses
of the same field, not separate singularity scenarios. Together they form the temporal Tower.
