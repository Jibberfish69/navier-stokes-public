\subsection{The balance at critical height}\label{sub:ThePerfectlyStretchingVortex}

Viscosity is supposed to save the fluid.
It smooths velocity differences, and it acts harder as those differences are packed into less space.
So the most direct way to solve the problem is to prove that anything trying to become singular eventually makes viscosity strong enough to stop it.

The problem is that a vortex can make the stretching stronger at the same time.
As the vortex stretches along its axis, incompressibility squeezes its cross-section.
The velocity turns across a thinner region, the gradients become steeper, and viscosity has more to work with.
But the same motion also intensifies the vorticity and the strain doing the stretching.
The vortex is strengthening the mechanism that sharpens it at the same time as the mechanism that smooths it.
A bend, a wobble, or a loss of alignment can break that repetition.
The motion we need to understand is the one that does not: the vortex remains aligned and stretches again.

Let
\[
  \omega:=\nabla\times u,
  \qquad
  S:=\frac12\bigl(\nabla u+(\nabla u)^{\mathsf T}\bigr).
\]
Taking the curl of Navier--Stokes gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:VorticityEquation}
  \partial_t\omega+(u\cdot\nabla)\omega
  =S\omega+\nu\Delta\omega.
\end{equation}
When \(\omega\) is aligned with a positive extensional direction of \(S\), the term \(S\omega\) amplifies the vorticity.
The same extension narrows the vortex and steepens the gradients seen by \(\nu\Delta\omega\).
The smaller vortex now poses one exact question: did viscosity gain anything from the contraction?

To compare the same normalized profile at the shorter length \(\ell/\lambda\), where \(\lambda>1\), use the Navier--Stokes rescaling
\[
  u_\lambda(x,t)=\lambda u(\lambda x,\lambda^2t),
  \qquad
  p_\lambda(x,t)=\lambda^2p(\lambda x,\lambda^2t).
\]
Each term in the momentum equation acquires the same factor:
\[
\begin{aligned}
  &\partial_tu_\lambda
  +(u_\lambda\cdot\nabla)u_\lambda
  +\nabla p_\lambda
  -\nu\Delta u_\lambda
  \\
  &\qquad=
  \lambda^3
  \bigl[
    \partial_tu+(u\cdot\nabla)u+\nabla p-\nu\Delta u
  \bigr](\lambda x,\lambda^2t).
\end{aligned}
\]
For the vortex variables,
\[
  \omega_\lambda(x,t)=\lambda^2\omega(\lambda x,\lambda^2t),
  \qquad
  S_\lambda(x,t)=\lambda^2S(\lambda x,\lambda^2t),
\]
and therefore
\[
  S_\lambda\omega_\lambda
  =\lambda^4(S\omega)(\lambda x,\lambda^2t),
  \qquad
  \nu\Delta\omega_\lambda
  =\lambda^4\nu(\Delta\omega)(\lambda x,\lambda^2t).
\]
Both sides of the contest strengthen by the same power of \(\lambda\).
This is a comparison between a profile and its exact rescaling, not a claim that the evolution must reproduce that profile.
What it establishes is the obstruction: if the vortex does reproduce the same normalized geometry, going smaller gives viscosity no relative advantage.

So what quantity can see that balance?
It has to give the smaller vortex the same size, because the contest inside the vortex has not changed.
For a homogeneous Sobolev norm,
\[
  \widehat{u_\lambda}(\xi,t)
  =\lambda^{-2}\widehat u(\xi/\lambda,\lambda^2t).
\]
With \(\eta=\xi/\lambda\),
\begin{align*}
  \norm{u_\lambda(t)}_{\dot H^s}^2
  &=\int_{\R^3}
  |\xi|^{2s}\lambda^{-4}
  \left|\widehat u(\xi/\lambda,\lambda^2t)\right|^2
  \,d\xi
  \\
  &=\lambda^{2s-1}
  \int_{\R^3}
  |\eta|^{2s}
  \left|\widehat u(\eta,\lambda^2t)\right|^2
  \,d\eta
  \\
  &=\lambda^{2s-1}
  \norm{u(\lambda^2t)}_{\dot H^s}^2.
\end{align*}
The exponent vanishes at one height:
\[
  s=\frac12.
\]
With \(\Lambda:=(-\Delta)^{1/2}\), define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:BodyCriticalHeightDefinition}
  H(t)
  :=\frac12\norm{u(t)}_{\dot H^{1/2}}^2
  =\frac12\norm{\Lambda^{1/2}u(t)}_2^2.
\end{equation}
This is the critical height.
Below \(\dot H^{1/2}\), concentration makes the norm smaller.
Above it, concentration makes the norm larger.
At \(\dot H^{1/2}\), the smaller vortex has exactly the same size.

Kinetic energy gives a completely different reading of the same contraction.
Pairing Navier--Stokes with \(u\), using incompressibility to remove transport and pressure, and integrating the viscous term by parts gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:BodyEnergyIdentity}
  \frac12\norm{u(t)}_2^2
  +\nu\int_0^t\norm{\nabla u(s)}_2^2\,ds
  =\frac12\norm{u_0}_2^2.
\end{equation}
Under the same concentration,
\[
  \norm{u_\lambda(t)}_2^2
  =\lambda^{-1}\norm{u(\lambda^2t)}_2^2,
  \qquad
  H_\lambda(t)=H(\lambda^2t).
\]
Kinetic energy falls by \(\lambda^{-1}\) while critical height does not fall at all.
The vortex becomes cheaper in the quantity controlled by the energy law without becoming any smaller at the height where stretching and viscosity remain equally matched.
The estimate can stay finite by losing track of where the concentration went.

Nothing in the energy law prevents another contraction.
Whether the vortex can keep repeating it now depends on time.
