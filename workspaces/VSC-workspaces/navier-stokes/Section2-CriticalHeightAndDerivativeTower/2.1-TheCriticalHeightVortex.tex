\subsection{The balance at critical height}\label{sub:ThePerfectlyStretchingVortex}

The obvious place to put your hope is viscosity.
The nonlinear term can stretch and concentrate the fluid, but viscosity is already there to smooth out the velocity differences that concentration creates.
So the direct idea is to show that, before those differences become singular, the viscous term takes over.
The obstruction appears when a vortex can make both sides stronger at once.

Stretch a vortex along its axis.
Incompressibility narrows its cross-section, so the velocity changes across that cross-section are packed into less space and the gradients become steeper.
Viscosity acts more strongly on those gradients.
The stretching also becomes stronger because the vorticity and strain have concentrated with them.
A bend, a wobble, or a loss of alignment can interrupt the repetition; the case that keeps the problem open is the vortex that remains aligned and stretches again.

Write
\[
  \omega:=\nabla\times u,
  \qquad
  S:=\frac12\bigl(\nabla u+(\nabla u)^{\mathsf T}\bigr).
\]
Taking the curl of Navier--Stokes gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:VorticityEquation}
  \partial_t\omega+(u\cdot\nabla)\omega
  =S\omega+\nu\Delta\omega.
\end{equation}
When \(\omega\) is aligned with a positive extensional direction of \(S\), the term \(S\omega\) amplifies the vorticity.
The same extension narrows the vortex and steepens the gradients seen by \(\nu\Delta\omega\).
The question is whether that narrowing gives viscosity any relative advantage.

Suppose one stretch replaces a characteristic length \(\ell\) by \(\ell/\lambda\), where \(\lambda>1\).
The corresponding Navier--Stokes rescaling is
\[
  u_\lambda(x,t)=\lambda u(\lambda x,\lambda^2t),
  \qquad
  p_\lambda(x,t)=\lambda^2p(\lambda x,\lambda^2t).
\]
Each term in the momentum equation acquires the same factor:
\[
\begin{aligned}
  &\partial_tu_\lambda
  +(u_\lambda\cdot\nabla)u_\lambda
  +\nabla p_\lambda
  -\nu\Delta u_\lambda
  \\
  &\qquad=
  \lambda^3
  \bigl[
    \partial_tu+(u\cdot\nabla)u+\nabla p-\nu\Delta u
  \bigr](\lambda x,\lambda^2t).
\end{aligned}
\]
For the vortex variables,
\[
  \omega_\lambda(x,t)=\lambda^2\omega(\lambda x,\lambda^2t),
  \qquad
  S_\lambda(x,t)=\lambda^2S(\lambda x,\lambda^2t),
\]
and therefore
\[
  S_\lambda\omega_\lambda
  =\lambda^4(S\omega)(\lambda x,\lambda^2t),
  \qquad
  \nu\Delta\omega_\lambda
  =\lambda^4\nu(\Delta\omega)(\lambda x,\lambda^2t).
\]
Both sides of the contest strengthen by the same power of \(\lambda\).
Scaling does not guarantee that the alignment persists, but it answers the question we asked: if the aligned vortex tightens and stretches again, going smaller does not improve viscosity's position.

This is where critical height enters.
If the vortex reproduces the same contest at a smaller scale, its measurement cannot shrink merely because the vortex did.
For a homogeneous Sobolev norm,
\[
  \widehat{u_\lambda}(\xi,t)
  =\lambda^{-2}\widehat u(\xi/\lambda,\lambda^2t).
\]
With \(\eta=\xi/\lambda\),
\begin{align*}
  \norm{u_\lambda(t)}_{\dot H^s}^2
  &=\int_{\R^3}
  |\xi|^{2s}\lambda^{-4}
  \left|\widehat u(\xi/\lambda,\lambda^2t)\right|^2
  \,d\xi
  \\
  &=\lambda^{2s-1}
  \int_{\R^3}
  |\eta|^{2s}
  \left|\widehat u(\eta,\lambda^2t)\right|^2
  \,d\eta
  \\
  &=\lambda^{2s-1}
  \norm{u(\lambda^2t)}_{\dot H^s}^2.
\end{align*}
The exponent vanishes at one height:
\[
  s=\frac12.
\]
With \(\Lambda:=(-\Delta)^{1/2}\), define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:BodyCriticalHeightDefinition}
  H(t)
  :=\frac12\norm{u(t)}_{\dot H^{1/2}}^2
  =\frac12\norm{\Lambda^{1/2}u(t)}_2^2.
\end{equation}
This is the critical height.
Below \(\dot H^{1/2}\), concentration makes the norm smaller; above it, concentration makes the norm larger; at \(\dot H^{1/2}\), the smaller vortex has exactly the same size.

Now compare that with the estimate supplied by kinetic energy.
Pairing Navier--Stokes with \(u\), using incompressibility to remove transport and pressure, and integrating the viscous term by parts gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:BodyEnergyIdentity}
  \frac12\norm{u(t)}_2^2
  +\nu\int_0^t\norm{\nabla u(s)}_2^2\,ds
  =\frac12\norm{u_0}_2^2.
\end{equation}
Under the same concentration,
\[
  \norm{u_\lambda(t)}_2^2
  =\lambda^{-1}\norm{u(\lambda^2t)}_2^2,
  \qquad
  H_\lambda(t)=H(\lambda^2t).
\]
Kinetic energy falls by \(\lambda^{-1}\) while critical height does not fall at all.
The vortex becomes cheaper in the quantity controlled by the energy law without becoming smaller in the quantity that measures the critical concentration.
That is why the energy law leaves this case open.

The balance can survive one change of scale.
The next question is how quickly the next scale can be produced.
