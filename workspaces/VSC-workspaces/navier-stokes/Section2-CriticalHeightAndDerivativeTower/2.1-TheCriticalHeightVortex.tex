\subsection{The critical-height vortex}\label{sub:ThePerfectlyStretchingVortex}

The whole regularity problem sits inside one balance.
A vortex stretches, its cross-section contracts, and its gradients sharpen.
Viscosity acts on those gradients.
If the vortex can repeat that motion at a smaller scale without giving viscosity a relative advantage, then every stretch returns the fluid to the same contest.

Take a vortex filament whose vorticity remains aligned with a positive extensional direction of the strain.
The strain stretches the filament and amplifies its vorticity.
Incompressibility narrows the cross-section as the filament lengthens, so the same motion sharpens the gradients on which viscosity acts.
A bend, a wobble, a loss of alignment, or a break in the filament interrupts the repetition.
The hard case is the motion that gives us none of those exits: the vortex stretches, narrows, and stays aligned to stretch again.

Write
\[
  \omega:=\nabla\times u,
  \qquad
  S:=\frac12\bigl(\nabla u+(\nabla u)^{\mathsf T}\bigr).
\]
Taking the curl of Navier--Stokes puts the stretching and viscous effects on the same line:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:VorticityEquation}
  \partial_t\omega+(u\cdot\nabla)\omega
  =S\omega+\nu\Delta\omega.
\end{equation}
When \(\omega\) points along a positive eigenvector of \(S\), the term \(S\omega\) amplifies it.
The contraction produced by that extension concentrates the vorticity, and \(\nu\Delta\omega\) diffuses the gradients created by the concentration.
Everything turns on whether making this vortex smaller gives diffusion a stronger relative position.

Let \(\lambda>1\), and replace a characteristic length \(\ell\) by \(\ell/\lambda\) through the Navier--Stokes rescaling
\[
  u_\lambda(x,t)=\lambda u(\lambda x,\lambda^2t),
  \qquad
  p_\lambda(x,t)=\lambda^2p(\lambda x,\lambda^2t).
\]
Substitution gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
  &\partial_tu_\lambda
  +(u_\lambda\cdot\nabla)u_\lambda
  +\nabla p_\lambda
  -\nu\Delta u_\lambda
  \\
  &\qquad=
  \lambda^3
  \bigl[
    \partial_tu+(u\cdot\nabla)u+\nabla p-\nu\Delta u
  \bigr](\lambda x,\lambda^2t).
\end{aligned}
\]
The concentrated fields therefore satisfy the same equation with the same viscosity.
For the vortex variables,
\[
  \omega_\lambda(x,t)=\lambda^2\omega(\lambda x,\lambda^2t),
  \qquad
  S_\lambda(x,t)=\lambda^2S(\lambda x,\lambda^2t),
\]
and hence
\[
  S_\lambda\omega_\lambda
  =\lambda^4(S\omega)(\lambda x,\lambda^2t),
  \qquad
  \nu\Delta\omega_\lambda
  =\lambda^4\nu(\Delta\omega)(\lambda x,\lambda^2t).
\]
Going smaller intensifies both terms by the same power.
Scaling does not hold the alignment in place, and it does not say that stretching and diffusion have equal magnitudes.
It says something more precise: if the aligned stretch repeats at a smaller scale, scale alone gives viscosity no advantage over it.

So which measurement sees the smaller vortex as the same problem?
For a homogeneous Sobolev norm,
\[
  \widehat{u_\lambda}(\xi,t)
  =\lambda^{-2}\widehat u(\xi/\lambda,\lambda^2t).
\]
With \(\eta=\xi/\lambda\),
\begin{align*}
  \norm{u_\lambda(t)}_{\dot H^s}^2
  &=\int_{\R^3}
  |\xi|^{2s}\lambda^{-4}
  \left|\widehat u(\xi/\lambda,\lambda^2t)\right|^2
  \,d\xi
  \\
  &=\lambda^{2s-1}
  \int_{\R^3}
  |\eta|^{2s}
  \left|\widehat u(\eta,\lambda^2t)\right|^2
  \,d\eta
  \\
  &=\lambda^{2s-1}
  \norm{u(\lambda^2t)}_{\dot H^s}^2.
\end{align*}
The factor disappears at one height and only one:
\[
  s=\frac12.
\]
With \(\Lambda:=(-\Delta)^{1/2}\), set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:BodyCriticalHeightDefinition}
  H(t)
  :=\frac12\norm{u(t)}_{\dot H^{1/2}}^2
  =\frac12\norm{\Lambda^{1/2}u(t)}_2^2.
\end{equation}
This is the critical height.
Norms below \(\dot H^{1/2}\) become smaller under concentration; norms above it become larger; \(H\) assigns the rescaled profile exactly the same size.
The vortex has tightened, but its critical measurement has not fallen.

The energy law sits below that height.
Pairing Navier--Stokes with \(u\), using incompressibility to remove transport and pressure, and integrating the viscous term by parts gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:BodyEnergyIdentity}
  \frac12\norm{u(t)}_2^2
  +\nu\int_0^t\norm{\nabla u(s)}_2^2\,ds
  =\frac12\norm{u_0}_2^2.
\end{equation}
For the same rescaled profile,
\[
  \norm{u_\lambda(t)}_2^2
  =\lambda^{-1}\norm{u(\lambda^2t)}_2^2,
  \qquad
  H_\lambda(t)=H(\lambda^2t).
\]
That is the gap.
The critical size survives the concentration while kinetic energy falls by the factor \(\lambda^{-1}\).
A narrowing vortex can fit more easily under the energy ceiling precisely while its critical danger stays unchanged.
That is why the energy law leaves the regularity problem open.

A critical-height vortex can now become smaller without paying more kinetic energy and without receiving any scale-based viscous rescue.
The remaining cost is time.
