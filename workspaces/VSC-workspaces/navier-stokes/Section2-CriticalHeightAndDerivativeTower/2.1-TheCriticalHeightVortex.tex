\subsection{The balance at critical height}\label{sub:ThePerfectlyStretchingVortex}

The first hope is that viscosity wins.
It keeps smoothing the velocity, so if that smoothing ever pulls ahead of the nonlinear motion, the sharpening has nowhere left to go.
But going to a smaller scale can strengthen both sides at once.
Viscosity can keep working harder without ever pulling ahead.

In three dimensions, vortex stretching is the motion that can keep the nonlinear side of that balance alive.
Take a material tube through a region where the vorticity is concentrated.
The tube follows fluid particles; because viscosity diffuses vorticity across particle labels, it should not be identified with a material vortex core for later times.
Incompressibility preserves the volume of that material tube, so a segment that lengthens must lose average transverse cross-sectional area.
The velocity then turns across a thinner region and its transverse gradients can become steeper.
Viscosity sees those steeper gradients immediately.
At the same time, positive extensional strain aligned with the vorticity amplifies the vorticity carried through the region.

None of this says that the alignment will survive, that the strain will keep its sign, or that one contraction will reproduce another.
Those are dynamical questions.
It identifies the dangerous case: enough aligned extension persists for the vortex to sharpen while viscosity is already acting on the same shrinking structure.

Fix any compact interval on which the solution remains classical.
Every curl, differentiation, and integration by parts below is made on that interval.

Let
\[
  \omega:=\nabla\times u,
  \qquad
  S:=\frac12\bigl(\nabla u+(\nabla u)^{\mathsf T}\bigr).
\]
Taking the curl of Navier--Stokes gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:VorticityEquation}
  \partial_t\omega+(u\cdot\nabla)\omega
  =S\omega+\nu\Delta\omega.
\end{equation}
The left side is the material rate of change of vorticity.
Taking the scalar product with \(\omega\) gives the pointwise balance
\[
  \frac12(\partial_t+u\cdot\nabla)|\omega|^2
  =\omega\cdot S\omega
  +\nu\,\omega\cdot\Delta\omega.
\]
When \(\omega\) is aligned with a positive extensional direction of \(S\), the term \(S\omega\) is the part that amplifies it.
The viscous term responds to the spatial variation of that vorticity.
Alignment can be lost, the extensional strain can weaken, and diffusion can erase the concentrated core.
The unresolved possibility is that the positive extension survives long enough for the core to sharpen again.
Before asking whether the evolution can realize that motion, we can ask the narrower question that scaling answers exactly: does smaller scale by itself give viscosity any advantage over stretching?

This comparison cannot be made by squeezing the width and holding everything else fixed.
The full Navier--Stokes rescaling changes the width, amplitude, and clock together.
For \(\lambda>1\), set
\[
  u_\lambda(x,t)=\lambda u(\lambda x,\lambda^2t),
  \qquad
  p_\lambda(x,t)=\lambda^2p(\lambda x,\lambda^2t).
\]
This produces a rescaled solution with rescaled initial data.
It does not assert that \(u_\lambda\) is a later state of the original history.
Each term in the momentum equation acquires the same factor:
\[
\begin{aligned}
  &\partial_tu_\lambda
  +(u_\lambda\cdot\nabla)u_\lambda
  +\nabla p_\lambda
  -\nu\Delta u_\lambda
  \\
  &\qquad=
  \lambda^3
  \bigl[
    \partial_tu+(u\cdot\nabla)u+\nabla p-\nu\Delta u
  \bigr](\lambda x,\lambda^2t).
\end{aligned}
\]
For the vortex variables,
\[
  \omega_\lambda(x,t)=\lambda^2\omega(\lambda x,\lambda^2t),
  \qquad
  S_\lambda(x,t)=\lambda^2S(\lambda x,\lambda^2t),
\]
so
\[
  S_\lambda\omega_\lambda
  =\lambda^4(S\omega)(\lambda x,\lambda^2t),
  \qquad
  \nu\Delta\omega_\lambda
  =\lambda^4\nu(\Delta\omega)(\lambda x,\lambda^2t).
\]
Both sides of the vorticity balance strengthen by the same power of \(\lambda\).
This is a homogeneity statement, not a recurrence theorem for the actual flow.
It says that contraction alone gives neither term a relative gain when the same normalized profile is viewed at a smaller scale.

The next question is how to measure a velocity field without mistaking a smaller copy for a weaker one.
The scale-invariant Sobolev norm supplies that measurement.
For a homogeneous Sobolev norm,
\[
  \widehat{u_\lambda}(\xi,t)
  =\lambda^{-2}\widehat u(\xi/\lambda,\lambda^2t).
\]
With \(\eta=\xi/\lambda\),
\begin{align*}
  \norm{u_\lambda(t)}_{\dot H^s}^2
  &=\int_{\R^3}
  |\xi|^{2s}\lambda^{-4}
  \left|\widehat u(\xi/\lambda,\lambda^2t)\right|^2
  \,d\xi
  \\
  &=\lambda^{2s-1}
  \int_{\R^3}
  |\eta|^{2s}
  \left|\widehat u(\eta,\lambda^2t)\right|^2
  \,d\eta
  \\
  &=\lambda^{2s-1}
  \norm{u(\lambda^2t)}_{\dot H^s}^2.
\end{align*}
Within the homogeneous \(L^2\)-Sobolev scale, the exponent vanishes at one and only one height:
\[
  s=\frac12.
\]
With \(\Lambda:=(-\Delta)^{1/2}\), define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:BodyCriticalHeightDefinition}
  H(t)
  :=\frac12\norm{u(t)}_{\dot H^{1/2}}^2
  =\frac12\norm{\Lambda^{1/2}u(t)}_2^2.
\end{equation}
We call this the critical height.
Below \(\dot H^{1/2}\), concentration makes the norm smaller.
Above it, concentration makes the norm larger.
At \(\dot H^{1/2}\), the smaller vortex has exactly the same size.

Kinetic energy reads the same contraction below critical height.
Pairing Navier--Stokes with \(u\), using incompressibility to remove transport and pressure, and integrating the viscous term by parts gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:BodyEnergyIdentity}
  \frac12\norm{u(t)}_2^2
  +\nu\int_0^t\norm{\nabla u(s)}_2^2\,ds
  =\frac12\norm{u_0}_2^2.
\end{equation}
Under the same concentration,
\[
  \norm{u_\lambda(t)}_2^2
  =\lambda^{-1}\norm{u(\lambda^2t)}_2^2,
  \qquad
  H_\lambda(t)=H(\lambda^2t).
\]
The kinetic energy falls by \(\lambda^{-1}\), while the critical height stays fixed.
A thinner rescaled profile can cost less energy and still retain its full critical size.
This compares solutions with rescaled data, not repeated cores inside one history; a core in the original flow must still carry its surrounding field and pressure.
Even with that distinction, the energy identity alone places no scale-independent ceiling on \(H\).

Scale has left stretching and viscosity level, and energy has failed to distinguish the smaller critical profile from the original one.
The comparison now has to move into time: how quickly does viscosity respond when the active length shrinks?
