\subsection{The perfectly stretching vortex}\label{sub:ThePerfectlyStretchingVortex}

Take one smooth vortex and stretch it along its axis.  Incompressibility will
not let the vortex gain length without losing cross-sectional area, so the core
narrows as it lengthens.  Its rotation is then carried through a smaller radius;
the winding tightens and the velocity changes more sharply across the core.
The next stretch does not begin from the original vortex.  It inherits the
thinner radius and the steeper variation left by the one before it.

Keep the axis aligned with the expanding direction of the strain and keep the
core centred as this process repeats.  This idealized motion is the perfectly
stretching vortex: every geometric advantage is given to stretching, and none
is lost to wobble or misalignment.  The picture tells us what to investigate;
only the equations will decide what the motion can actually sustain.

Now suppose, for contradiction, that the maximal classical solution has a
finite last time.  Write
\[
  T_*<\infty,
  \qquad
  I_*:=(0,T_*).
\]
The solution is smooth on every closed interval contained in \(I_*\).  Whatever
prevents continuation must therefore occur in the approach
\(t\uparrow T_*\), precisely where the repeated narrowing would have to
accumulate.

The word \emph{core} hides a choice that cannot be postponed.  It may follow
the same material particles throughout the narrowing, or it may name a visible
region that recruits new particles as earlier ones leave.  In the first reading
we must track the separation of fixed fluid labels.  In the second we must
track concentration in the complete velocity field.  The same drawing suggests
both stories, but they make different mathematical claims.

Write \(\omega:=\nabla\times u\) and
\(S:=\tfrac12\bigl(\nabla u+(\nabla u)^{\mathsf T}\bigr)\).
Taking the curl of \eqref{eq:NavierStokesSystem} and using incompressibility gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:VorticityEquation}
  D_t\omega
  :=\partial_t\omega+(u\cdot\nabla)\omega
  =S\omega+\nu\Delta\omega.
\end{equation}
The alignment in our thought experiment makes \(S\omega\) amplify the
vorticity.  Incompressibility supplies the transverse contraction, while the
Laplacian acts on the sharper curvature created by that contraction.  Taking
the curl has removed pressure from the displayed equation, not from the fluid:
pressure still helps determine the divergence-free velocity and strain that do
the stretching.  Amplification and diffusion are therefore acting on the same
narrowing vortex at the same time.

The drawing is local, whereas a finite maximal time concerns the whole
solution.  The bridge is the endpoint theorem of Escauriaza, Seregin, and
\v{S}ver\'ak: a bounded \(L^\infty_tL^3_x\) norm carries a solution regularly
through the endpoint \parencite{EscauriazaSereginSverak2003}.  Our assumed
finite maximal time must therefore force
\begin{equation}\label{eq:FiniteTimeLThreeEscape}
  T_*<\infty
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \sup_{0<t<T_*}\norm{u(t)}_3=\infty.
\end{equation}
This is the precise velocity escape used in the paper: the global \(L^3\)
norm exceeds every finite bound.  It does not say that the pointwise velocity
must diverge at the centre drawn in
\cref{fig:BalancedVortexCriticalRoute}; the criterion deliberately leaves that
location open.

A shrinking radius by itself proves too little, because a rescaled copy of one
fixed profile also looks thinner.  We need a quantity that does not change
under that rescaling but is nevertheless forced to grow along the actual
history.  That quantity is the critical height.
