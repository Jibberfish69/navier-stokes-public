\subsection{The critical-height problem}\label{sub:ThePerfectlyStretchingVortex}

The hard case begins where vortex stretching and viscosity scale exactly alike.
Take a vortex whose vorticity remains aligned with a positive extensional direction of the strain.
The strain amplifies the vorticity, incompressibility contracts the transverse scale of the stretched region, and viscosity acts on the sharper gradients created by that contraction.
Real vortices can bend, wobble, fragment, or lose the alignment that feeds the stretch.
The case the proof has to cover is the one that keeps the alignment and continues to concentrate.

Write
\[
  \omega:=\nabla\times u,
  \qquad
  S:=\frac12\bigl(\nabla u+(\nabla u)^{\mathsf T}\bigr).
\]
Taking the curl of Navier--Stokes gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:VorticityEquation}
  \partial_t\omega+(u\cdot\nabla)\omega
  =S\omega+\nu\Delta\omega.
\end{equation}
When \(\omega\) points along an eigenvector of \(S\) with positive eigenvalue, \(S\omega\) strengthens it.
For an aligned filamentary vortex, that extension is accompanied by transverse contraction.
If \(\ell\) denotes the active transverse length, the next sharper configuration replaces \(\ell\) by \(\ell/\lambda\) for some \(\lambda>1\).
The immediate issue is whether this change of scale favors the stretching term or the viscous term.

Navier--Stokes answers through its natural rescaling:
\[
  u_\lambda(x,t)=\lambda u(\lambda x,\lambda^2t),
  \qquad
  p_\lambda(x,t)=\lambda^2p(\lambda x,\lambda^2t).
\]
The spatial argument \(\lambda x\) contracts every length by \(\lambda^{-1}\).
At the same time,
\[
\begin{aligned}
  \partial_tu_\lambda
  &=\lambda^3(\partial_tu)(\lambda x,\lambda^2t),
  &
  (u_\lambda\cdot\nabla)u_\lambda
  &=\lambda^3((u\cdot\nabla)u)(\lambda x,\lambda^2t),
  \\
  \nabla p_\lambda
  &=\lambda^3(\nabla p)(\lambda x,\lambda^2t),
  &
  \nu\Delta u_\lambda
  &=\lambda^3\nu(\Delta u)(\lambda x,\lambda^2t).
\end{aligned}
\]
The nonlinear stretching and viscous response carry the same factor \(\lambda^3\).
Concentration gives neither term a scaling advantage.
That equality of scale weight is the critical balance.

The corresponding Sobolev height follows from the same rescaling.
Since
\[
  \widehat{u_\lambda}(\xi,t)
  =\lambda^{-2}\widehat u(\xi/\lambda,\lambda^2t),
\]
the change of variables \(\eta=\xi/\lambda\) gives
\begin{align*}
  \norm{u_\lambda(t)}_{\dot H^s}^2
  &=
  \int_{\R^3}
  |\xi|^{2s}\lambda^{-4}
  \left|\widehat u(\xi/\lambda,\lambda^2t)\right|^2
  \,d\xi
  \\
  &=
  \lambda^{2s-1}
  \int_{\R^3}
  |\eta|^{2s}
  \left|\widehat u(\eta,\lambda^2t)\right|^2
  \,d\eta.
\end{align*}
Hence
\[
  \norm{u_\lambda(t)}_{\dot H^s}
  =\lambda^{s-1/2}\norm{u(\lambda^2t)}_{\dot H^s}.
\]
The exponent vanishes only at \(s=\tfrac12\).
With \(\Lambda:=(-\Delta)^{1/2}\), set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:BodyCriticalHeightDefinition}
  H(t)
  :=\frac12\norm{u(t)}_{\dot H^{1/2}}^2
  =\frac12\norm{\Lambda^{1/2}u(t)}_2^2.
\end{equation}
This is the critical height: a rescaled configuration can move to a shorter length while keeping exactly the same value of \(H\).

The kinetic energy sits below this height.
At \(s=0\),
\[
  \norm{u_\lambda(t)}_2^2
  =\lambda^{-1}\norm{u(\lambda^2t)}_2^2.
\]
The energy identity itself comes from pairing Navier--Stokes with \(u\).
Incompressibility cancels the transport and pressure pairings, and integration by parts supplies the viscous sign:
\[
  \frac12\frac{d}{dt}\norm{u(t)}_2^2
  +\nu\norm{\nabla u(t)}_2^2
  =0.
\]
After integration in time,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:BodyEnergyIdentity}
  \frac12\norm{u(t)}_2^2
  +\nu\int_0^t\norm{\nabla u(s)}_2^2\,ds
  =\frac12\norm{u_0}_2^2.
\end{equation}
This law controls the total kinetic energy.
Under concentration, however, the rescaled configuration carries \(\lambda^{-1}\) of that energy and the same critical height.
An energy bound can hold across an arbitrarily concentrated scale family because the quantity it controls becomes smaller precisely where \(H\) remains unchanged.
That is why the energy law leaves the critical-height problem open.

Once energy leaves this concentration open, the next issue is how much time viscosity has to act at the new scale.
A structure of length \(\ell\) has the linear viscous smoothing time
\begin{equation}\label{eq:BodyHeatComparisonTime}
  \delta t_{\mathrm{heat}}(\ell)\asymp\frac{\ell^2}{\nu}.
\end{equation}
This is the response time of \(\nu\Delta\) at scale \(\ell\); the nonlinear evolution still has to produce the next structure.
Replacing \(\ell\) by \(\ell/\lambda\) divides the heat time by \(\lambda^2\), exactly the time factor already present in \(u_\lambda(x,t)\).

Prescribe a geometric scale family
\[
  \ell_k=\rho^k\ell_0,
  \qquad 0<\rho<1.
\]
If the comparison constants in \eqref{eq:BodyHeatComparisonTime} are uniform across this family, then
\[
  \delta t_{k}^{\mathrm{heat}}
  \asymp\frac{\ell_0^2}{\nu}\rho^{2k},
  \qquad
  \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\delta t_k^{\mathrm{heat}}
  \asymp
  \frac{\ell_0^2}{\nu}\frac{1}{1-\rho^2}.
\]
The sum is finite.
This is the Zeno comparison clock: smaller spatial scales come with a summable sequence of viscous response times.

The same rescaling sharpens every spatial derivative.
For \(m\in\Nzero\),
\[
  \nabla^m u_\lambda(x,t)
  =\lambda^{m+1}(\nabla^m u)(\lambda x,\lambda^2t).
\]
In particular, one scale step multiplies the velocity gradient by \(\lambda^2\) while dividing the heat-comparison time by \(\lambda^2\).
Across the rescaled family, the clock runs downward as the gradients run upward.
The limiting comparison profile is the singularity direction carried by the stretching vortex.

The evolving solution becomes a Zeno cascade only by realizing that comparison family.
It has to generate the successive scales \(\ell_k\), make the transitions on intervals tied to those scales, and accumulate the transition times at a common limit \(\tau\).
Critical-height growth can be recorded independently.
If \(H\) crosses arbitrarily large levels, choose \(H_0>H(0)\) and define
\[
  L_k:=2^kH_0,
  \qquad
  t_k:=\inf\{t>0:H(t)=L_k\}.
\]
Continuity gives \(t_0<t_1<t_2<\cdots\).
These first passages record what the actual solution does in the critical norm; the lengths \(\ell_k\) and heat times \(\delta t_k^{\mathrm{heat}}\) record the scale comparison.
A realized critical-height Zeno route requires a bridge between them, together with \(t_k\uparrow\tau\).

That requirement is the pivot into the derivative Tower.
A cascade is a sequence of changes made by the one evolving velocity field, not a collection of rescaled pictures.
If distinguishable velocity states are produced across intervals that shrink to zero, the time response has to grow.
Acceleration, jerk, and the later temporal responses enter because they are what generate those changes.
