\subsection{Why finite-time escape opens the temporal Tower}\label{sub:BodyFiniteTimeResponseEscalation}

Suppose \(\norm{u(t)}_3\) escapes before \(T_*\).
If \(u_t\) stayed uniformly bounded in \(L^3\), then
\[
  u(t)=u_0+\int_0^t u_t(s)\,ds
\]
would keep the velocity bounded on that finite interval.
If \(u_{tt}\) stayed bounded, one integration would give
\[
  u_t(t)=u_t(0)+\int_0^t u_{tt}(s)\,ds
\]
and the first integral would again bound \(u\).
At any higher fixed order, integrating down the derivative chain finitely many times gives the same conclusion.

This is why the singularity itself opens a temporal Tower.
At fixed spatial coordinates, write
\begin{equation}\label{eq:TemporalTowerDefinitionBody}
  V_n:=\partial_t^nu,
  \qquad
  Q_n:=\partial_t^np,
  \qquad n\in\Nzero.
\end{equation}
At arbitrary order, the same integration is the Banach-valued Taylor formula.

\begin{lemma}[Repeated finite-time integration]\label{lem:RepeatedFiniteTimeIntegration}
Let \(n\geq1\), and let \(f:[0,T_*)\to X\) have the derivatives required by the integral Taylor formula in a Banach space \(X\).
For \(0<t<T_*\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RepeatedFiniteTimeBound}
  \norm{f(t)}_X
  \leq
  \sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{t^j}{j!}\norm{\partial_t^jf(0)}_X
  +\frac{t^n}{n!}\norm{\partial_t^nf}_{L^\infty(0,T_*;X)}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The integral Taylor formula gives
\[
  f(t)
  =\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{t^j}{j!}\partial_t^jf(0)
  +\frac1{(n-1)!}\int_0^t(t-s)^{n-1}\partial_t^nf(s)\,ds.
\]
Taking the \(X\)-norm and bounding the integral by its essential supremum gives \eqref{eq:RepeatedFiniteTimeBound}.
\end{proof}

Apply the lemma with \(f=u\) and \(X=L^3(\R^3)\).
The smooth datum supplies every finite initial time derivative, and \(T_*<\infty\) keeps every polynomial factor finite.
The escape in \eqref{eq:FiniteTimeLThreeEscape} can now happen only if, for each fixed \(n\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:TemporalTowerMustEscape}
  \sup_{0<t<T_*}\norm{V_n(t)}_3=\infty.
\end{equation}
For each finite \(n\), the row \(V_n\) is unbounded in \(L^3\).
Each order may escape at a different time, place, and rate; the lemma rules out boundedness separately in every fixed row.
The Tower is
\[
  u,\quad u_t,\quad u_{tt},\quad u_{ttt},\quad\ldots.
\]
The first two rows invite the physical words acceleration and jerk, but a moving fluid particle experiences something different.
If \(X(a,t)\) is the trajectory beginning at the material label \(a\), then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ParticleTrajectory}
  \frac d{dt}X(a,t)=u(X(a,t),t),
  \qquad
  X(a,0)=a.
\end{equation}
The velocity seen by that moving particle changes according to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:MaterialAccelerationBody}
  \frac d{dt}u(X(a,t),t)
  =\partial_tu+(u\cdot\nabla)u
  =:D_tu.
\end{equation}
The equation gives that material acceleration as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:MaterialAccelerationEquationBody}
  D_tu=-\nabla p+\nu\Delta u.
\end{equation}
Applying \(D_t=\partial_t+u\cdot\nabla\) once more produces \((\partial_t+u\cdot\nabla)(-\nabla p+\nu\Delta u)\).
The material response already combines time derivatives with spatial derivatives of pressure and velocity.
Expanding that second material derivative shows the mixing directly:
\[
  \begin{aligned}
  D_t^2u
  ={}&V_2+(V_1\cdot\nabla)V_0+2(V_0\cdot\nabla)V_1
  \\
  &+(V_0\cdot\nabla)\bigl((V_0\cdot\nabla)V_0\bigr).
  \end{aligned}
\]
Even a spatial derivative changes when it passes through \(D_t\):
\[
  [D_t,\partial_\ell]f
  =-(\partial_\ell u\cdot\nabla)f.
\]
The material and fixed-position descriptions are two readings of the same history.
The particle view records what the moving fluid experiences; the fixed-position rows keep temporal order separate from the spatial derivatives entering that experience.

At a fixed spatial point, the first row is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:BodyFirstTemporalVelocityRow}
  V_1+(V_0\cdot\nabla)V_0+\nabla Q_0
  =\nu\Delta V_0.
\end{equation}
Differentiating once gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:BodySecondTemporalVelocityRow}
  V_2
  +(V_1\cdot\nabla)V_0
  +(V_0\cdot\nabla)V_1
  +\nabla Q_1
  =\nu\Delta V_1.
\end{equation}
The first nonlinear term changes the velocity doing the carrying.
The second changes the response carried through the existing flow.
The right side contains two additional spatial derivatives of the current row.
After \(n\) differentiations, the same division is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:BodyTemporalVelocityRecurrence}
  V_{n+1}
  +\sum_{a=0}^{n}\binom{n}{a}(V_a\cdot\nabla)V_{n-a}
  +\nabla Q_n
  =\nu\Delta V_n,
  \qquad
  \nabla\cdot V_n=0.
\end{equation}
Incompressibility fixes the pressure response from these same velocity rows:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:PressureNormalization}
  -\Delta p=\partial_i\partial_j(u_i u_j),
  \qquad
  p=R_iR_j(u_i u_j),
\end{equation}
with the decaying whole-space normalization.
Time differentiation gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:BodyTemporalPressureRecurrence}
  Q_n
  =R_iR_j\sum_{a=0}^{n}\binom{n}{a}(V_a)_i(V_{n-a})_j.
\end{equation}
Each temporal row already contains the spatial direction: \(\Delta V_n\) contributes two spatial derivatives, while pressure is rebuilt from products of the same rows.
The temporal index alone cannot say which spatial derivative inside that response is being used.
The recurrence has forced a second coordinate, obtained by differentiating these equations in space.
