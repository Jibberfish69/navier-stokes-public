The Three-Dimensional Navier–Stokes Regularity
                  Problem
                                  Thomas Birnie
                                 August 16, 2026


                                       Abstract
    We prove global existence and smoothness for the unforced three-dimensional
incompressible Navier–Stokes equations on R3 , for every viscosity ν > 0 and every
divergence-free Schwartz initial velocity. Assume that the maximal classical time T∗ is
finite. The energy identity keeps kinetic energy bounded, but endpoint continuation
forces the critical L3 norm and the scale-invariant height
                                       1
                                 H(t) = ∥Λ1/2 u(t)∥22
                                       2
past every finite bound. In the perfectly stretching vortex picture, the fluid would
have to pass through infinitely many doubled critical-height levels before that one last
time. Repeated integration then forces every fixed temporal derivative of the velocity
to become unbounded in the same critical space.
    Differentiating the equation generates the full temporal and spatial derivative Tower
together with its pressure response. In every temporal row, the viscous Laplacian
contributes two further spatial derivatives, and the critical-height identities expose
successively higher Sobolev energies. A datum-generated estimate controls every finite
adjacent rectangle of the Tower uniformly on (0, T∗ ). Every larger rectangle restricts
to the same derivatives in the smaller ones, so all of them belong to one intersection
for the original velocity and pressure. The lowest rectangles bound the critical norm,
critical height, stretching action, and high-frequency tail, while the full intersection
gives one terminal velocity in H ∞ . Local well-posedness and uniqueness continue that
same history beyond its supposed last time, contradicting maximality. Thus T∗ = ∞;
velocity and normalized pressure remain smooth for all time, and kinetic energy stays
bounded by its initial value.

1     The Regularity Problem
Fefferman’s whole-space problem fixes ν > 0, starts from a smooth divergence-free velocity
u0 on R3 whose derivatives decay faster than every power at infinity, and asks whether the
Navier–Stokes evolution from that datum stays smooth for all time (Fefferman 2000). In the
notation used here,

                                          u(·, 0) = u0

The nonlinear term transports velocity through its own field, pressure enforces the divergence
constraint across that field, and viscosity diffuses spatial variation. We take the pressure with
its decaying whole-space normalization. The problem is to prove that this one pair exists for
every t ≥ 0, with

global conclusion above is exactly the statement

2     Critical Height and the Derivative Tower
Assume the opposite. Let the maximal classical history have a finite last time and write

                                  T∗ < ∞,      I∗ := (0, T∗ ).

The solution is smooth on every closed subinterval of I∗ , so every possible loss of smoothness
is confined to the approach t ↑ T∗ . Begin with a vortex that remains in the geometry most
favourable to stretching.

2.1    The perfectly stretching vortex
Take one smooth vortex and stretch it along its axis. The fluid is incompressible, so the
lengthening contracts the cross-section at the same time. The rotation is packed into a
smaller radius, the winding tightens, and the velocity changes more sharply across the core.
The next stretch begins from that thinner vortex, with the steeper variation already in place.
    Now assume that its axis stays aligned with the expanding strain direction and that
the core remains centred and coherent from one stretch to the next. This is the perfectly
stretching vortex. It is a thought experiment that isolates the geometry most favourable to
repeated concentration; the equations below carry every exact implication.
    Before going any further, we have to decide what the word core is following. It may
contain the same material particles throughout the narrowing, or it may be a visible region
that recruits new particles while earlier ones leave. The first reading concerns the separation
of fixed fluid labels. The second concerns concentration in the complete velocity field. The
drawing is identical in both readings, but the mathematical claims differ.

    Continuity now converts that unbounded height into a sequence of times. The solution
is classical before the endpoint, so H is continuous on [0, T ] for every T < T∗ . Choose
H0 > H(0), set Lk = 2k H0 , and let

                               t0 := inf{t ∈ (0, T∗ ) : H(t) = H0 },

                             tk+1 := inf{t ∈ (tk , T∗ ) : H(t) = Lk+1 }.

                  t0 < t1 < t2 < · · · < T∗ , H(tk ) = 2k H0 ,   tk ↑ T∗ .             (2.2.7)
Any accumulation time must be T∗ : an earlier limit would lie inside the smooth interval,
where H is locally bounded.
    This is the Zeno cascade. The same fluid crosses infinitely many doubled critical-height
levels before one finite last time. Between those crossings, H may rise or fall; the construction
records only the first time each new level is reached. The same finite endpoint also forces
∥u(t)∥3 to be unbounded. Repeated integration carries that escape through every fixed
temporal row.
